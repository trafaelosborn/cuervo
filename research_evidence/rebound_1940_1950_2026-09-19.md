# Twenty-two people: explaining Cuervo's 1940-1950 rebound

*Research brief for Taylor Osborn, September 19, 2026. Cuervo precinct, including its surrounding rural territory.*

## 1. The most defensible SWAG

**Cuervo's increase from 188 to 210 looks compatible with ordinary family turnover, household formation, and a handful of arrivals in a still-working ranching and railroad community. A substantial county cattle-market expansion makes an economic contribution plausible. The evidence does not yet assign the 22-person gain to the war.**

The gain was **11.7% of the 1940 population**, but only 22 people over ten years. Both facts matter. This is a small population with volatile totals; the published census counts are nominally whole-population counts, so the central issue is not ordinary survey sampling error.

- **More, smaller households:** manual grouping of the complete retained schedules gives 42 head-listed households in 1940 and 59 in 1950. Mean size falls from 4.48 to 3.56. [C40, C50]
- **Recent arrivals are visible:** seven supplementary entries name a different place of residence one year earlier. They belong to six households; their other members are not automatically proven migrants. [C50]
- **The cattle economy did expand:** Guadalupe County cattle and calf sales rose from 7,399 head in 1939 to 19,724 in 1944, then eased to 17,595 in 1949. These are animals sold, not inflated dollar totals. [A45, A50]

![Household counts and cattle sales. The two panels describe different geographies and measures; they are evidence to connect, not a demonstrated causal relationship.](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/rebound_1940_1950_2026-09-19/households_and_cattle.png)

**What this changes for the book:** a declining place can remain economically active and receive new families. A modest census rebound need not mark a durable recovery, and it does not erase the earlier decline.

## 2. What the demographic arithmetic actually says

There are **51 children under ten in the 1950 schedule**, leaving **159 residents aged ten or older**. Thus the observed totals can be written:

**188 - 29 + 51 = 210.**

The birth cohorts already alive at the 1940 census are represented by 29 fewer people in 1950, while 51 children born in the following ten years are present. This is an accounting identity conditional on the census readings and comparable coverage, not an estimate of births, deaths, or migration. [C40, C50]

**It does not mean 51 local births and 29 departures.** Some children arrived from elsewhere. Some older residents were newcomers. Deaths, moves, reporting errors, and any territorial differences are mixed into the older-cohort balance. Births followed by death or departure are invisible in the 1950 child count.

### A change in household structure

| Measure | 1940 | 1950 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Named residents | 188 | 210 |
| Head-listed household groups | 42 | 59 |
| Mean residents per group | 4.48 | 3.56 |
| One- or two-person groups | 13 | 27 |
| Known children under ten | 52 | 51 |
| Known children under eighteen | 90 | 85 |
| Median of readable ages | 18 | 25.5 |

One 1940 age remains unread. It can add at most one to the corresponding age-group counts; it does not affect total population or household grouping. Two adult-age corrections underpin the revised 1950 median; see section 8.

The household count rises **40.5%**, while mean size falls **20.5%**. New arrivals, young adults setting up separate households, and household reorganization could all contribute. These figures are not a count of 17 new houses or 17 immigrant families.

There is also a useful cohort clue: the **102 known residents aged 0-19 in 1940** correspond to only **69 residents aged 10-29 in 1950**. Conversely, the known age-40-59 count of 26 becomes an age-50-69 count of 36. These are unmatched cohorts, not traced individuals. Nevertheless, the opposing changes make uniform retention plus births an inadequate description of the records.

## 4. Arrivals close to the 1950 census

The supplementary questions ask selected residents where they lived one year earlier. The following explicit place entries survive. They give unusually useful timing: some movement occurred after the war and shortly before enumeration. [C50, questions 21-24]

| Resident and 1950 age | Prior residence written | Sheet:line |
|---|---|---|
| Mike Wood, 25 | California; county/place blank | 1:3 |
| Mary Simpson, 21 | Tatum, Lea County, New Mexico | 1:8 |
| Laura Beth Simpson, 7 | Tatum, Lea County, New Mexico | 1:13 |
| Vaughn Ray Simpson, 10 | Texas; county/place overwritten and unresolved | 1:18 |
| Nicholas Aragon, 25 | Tucumcari, Quay County, New Mexico | 6:11 |
| Pauline Aragon, 1 | Tucumcari, Quay County, New Mexico | 6:16 |
| Esther L. Lack, 30 | Santa Rosa, Guadalupe County, New Mexico | 7:5 |

The address fields are clearer than some checkboxes. Mike Wood's same-house answer shows an earlier mark; Laura Beth's and Pauline's boxes are overwritten. Esther's county/place is written despite blank same-county boxes. These imperfections are retained in the transcription notes rather than treated as clean questionnaire routing.

**Scale check:** the six entries naming places outside Guadalupe County belong to five households containing 23 people in 1950. Including Esther Lack gives seven entries in six households containing 25 people. That shows how few family units could matter to a net change of 22. **It does not prove that all 23 or 25 moved together, or that these households caused the net gain.**

Wood was listed as a ranch hired hand. The three Simpson households have ranching occupation entries. Nicholas Aragon's main occupation is railroad work. That is a direct association between recent movers and these sectors, although their reasons for moving are not recorded.

Esther Lack's prior Santa Rosa residence also cautions against treating movement as uniformly toward the larger place. It is one observed reverse move, not evidence that Cuervo generally drew population from Santa Rosa.

These supplementary lines are a sample, not a complete list of movers. No multiplication by five or estimate of a precinct migration rate is warranted from this small, partly overwritten set. A person could also have returned after an earlier absence.

## 5. Children, ranch families, and wartime service

**The child-heavy population is real. The inference that Cuervo was therefore spared wartime losses needs more work.** In 1940, at least 90 of its 188 residents were under eighteen, but some adolescents would become military-age adults during the war. Age at the 1940 census is not a service record. [C40]

The 1950 supplementary questions contain **five affirmative answers for service during World War II**. This is a minimum observed count among people present in 1950, not a count of everyone who served from Cuervo. [C50, question 33a]

| Name as read | Age in 1950 | Sheet:line | Earlier Cuervo candidate |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Henry Yates | 34 | 1:23 | Henry, 24, in parents' 1940 household |
| Jose Quintana | 27 | 3:1 | Jose, 16, with Manuel and Quiteria in 1940 |
| Bernice Kester | 40 | 5:14 | Burnice Kester, 31, with Cordellie in 1940 |
| Nicholas Aragon | 25 | 6:11 | Not linked in this pass |
| Harold R. Boyle | 26 | 71:2 | Not linked in this pass |

The Kester spelling varies between readings. These three earlier identity candidates are supported by family context but remain candidates. The service answers do not identify induction versus enlistment, dates away, combat, casualties, or when a veteran returned. They also omit veterans who had died or lived elsewhere by 1950.

### Were the children especially concentrated in ranch families?

Using the existing 1950 occupation readings, **24 households have at least one ranching occupation entry**. They contain **91 people**, including **18 children under ten and 33 under eighteen**. Across the whole precinct there are 51 under-ten children and 85 under-eighteen children. [C50; derived join]

Thus ranch-associated households contain about **43% of residents but 35% of the under-ten children**. Large families also appear in households associated with railroad work, construction, and postal employment. The nine-person Alfonso Chavez household has no main occupation entry available for this classification.

This is not a fertility study: a 1950 occupation does not establish a parent's occupation when children were born, and an adult child's departure changes the household snapshot. It does mean that **the surviving evidence does not support assigning the entire rebound to unusually large ranch families**.

The more productive military question is whether service, return, marriage, and household formation affected particular families. A comparative claim about fewer draftees than Santa Rosa still needs matching age denominators and actual service evidence for both places.

## 6. The wartime cattle hypothesis gets a real test

Newly acquired agricultural census tables give three observations for the whole county. They support a substantial expansion in cattle inventory and marketed animals. They do not identify Cuervo's share. [A45, A50]

| Guadalupe County measure | Earlier observation | Wartime observation | Later observation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cattle/calves sold alive, head | 7,399 (1939) | 19,724 (1944) | 17,595 (1949) |
| Cattle/calves on farms, head | 19,227 (1940) | 27,047 (1945) | 41,276 (1950) |
| Farms reporting cattle inventory | 370 (1940) | 481 (1945) | 325 (1950) |
| All counted farms | 476 (1940) | 640 (1945) | 381 (1950) |
| Average farm size, acres | 2,023.2 (1940) | 2,370.9 (1945) | 4,796.0 (1950) |

**The strongest comparison is sales:** the number of cattle and calves sold rose **166.6% from 1939 to 1944**. It fell **10.8% from 1944 to 1949**, remaining **137.8% above 1939**. Prices alone cannot account for an increase measured in head, although price incentives could affect the quantity sold.

This gives the hypothesis a local economic basis: a larger cattle market could support work, income, and demand for services in ranching communities. The recent ranch-associated residents in section 4 provide people whose employment and moves could be investigated next.

**The missing connection is still consequential:** animals sold in Guadalupe County are not automatically cattle shipped from Cuervo, shipments by rail, cattle sent to the coasts, or military purchases. Sales may also include animals bought and resold. Neither these tables nor resident occupation entries establish additional Cuervo railroad jobs during the war.

### Comparability checks that change the interpretation

- Inventory dates differ: **April 1, 1940; January 1, 1945; approximately April 1, 1950**, with the county's 1950 average enumeration period reported as April 15-28. Seasonal differences can affect herd comparisons. Sales refer to whole calendar years. [A45 footnote; A50; A50D]
- The **1950 farm definition changed** and excluded some small operations previously counted. The fall from 640 to 381 farms cannot all be called ranch consolidation. The census introduction says the definition change had little effect on aggregate livestock/crop totals, but does not supply a Cuervo-specific adjustment. [A50D]
- Weather, herd rebuilding, changes in stock management, and market conditions are rival explanations for cattle growth. Wartime timing is suggestive, not a controlled causal comparison.

More cattle can coexist with fewer farms and fewer people. The agricultural series strengthens the economic hypothesis while also explaining why a stronger cattle economy need not reverse regional depopulation.

## 7. Working explanations

These rankings express fit to the available records, not numerical probabilities or significance tests.

### 1. Family turnover and arrivals - strongest account

More small households, changing membership within continuing families, and recent arrivals could yield a 22-person gain while others left or died. Exhaustive person linkage is needed to apportion these components.

### 2. Support from cattle activity - plausible

County cattle sales expanded during the war and remained elevated afterward. Several recent movers lived in ranch-associated households. Actual hiring, ranch operations, and reasons for moving remain the missing links; no specific number of Cuervo residents is explained yet.

### 3. Ordinary volatility - still viable

A few household moves, family births, deaths, or enumeration differences could alter a population near 200 substantially. Such events have causes even when they do not reflect a durable economic turn. Without an annual series, the 1950 point cannot tell us whether the population peaked in 1943, 1949, or never followed a smooth path at all. We cannot assign a defensible probability that the gain was "random."

### 4. Differences in wartime service - weakly tested

A child-heavy population could have differed from Santa Rosa in wartime exposure, but the comparison is unfinished and five 1950 residents explicitly reported WWII service. Service alone also does not measure permanent population loss. This hypothesis deserves a bounded follow-up rather than becoming the explanation by default.

### Provisional wording for the monograph

Cuervo's population rose from 188 in 1940 to 210 in 1950, a modest interruption of its earlier decline. The household schedules reveal a changing community: more, smaller households, continuing families whose children had grown or left, and recent arrivals associated with ranch and railroad work. Across Guadalupe County, cattle sales had expanded sharply during the war and remained well above their prewar level in 1949. That expansion may have helped sustain Cuervo, but it has not been shown to account for the increase. Twenty-two additional residents could emerge from only a handful of family histories; the census rebound alone cannot establish an economic recovery.

### The next evidence with the highest payoff

1. **Date the moves:** trace the three Simpson households, the Aragons, and the Lacks through 1940 schedules, directories, school admissions, and other dated records. Record return migration as well as first arrival.
2. **Resolve the largest continuing-family changes:** follow the six Trujillo sons and the Yates children. Establish destinations and dates before attributing absences to death or service.
3. **Find the economic connection:** seek named ranch employment, stock-shipment, station-revenue, and railroad payroll records. A Cuervo-specific annual school or postal series could distinguish a temporary spike from sustained growth.

### Audit notes

All **398 census appearances** now have first-pass name readings, ages carried from the earlier inventory, and grouping into **101 head-listed households**. These are appearances, not 398 distinct people. Grouping follows head entries and continuations rather than dwelling numbers alone. Two separately head-listed groups share one 1950 dwelling serial. Vacant and no-one-home placeholders are excluded; callback sheet 71 is included. Household comparability still needs checking against enumeration definitions.

**Two clear 1950 age-reading corrections:** Ruth Parker, sheet 1 line 2, is **38**, not 58; Henry Yates, sheet 1 line 23, is **34**, not 24. The revised median is **25.5**, previously 25. Neither correction changes population or child counts. The original September 18 data and PDF remain dated audit copies; this brief and its revised person file supersede those two age readings. Other handwritten entries remain subject to review.

This is complete name/group coverage and **selected linkage**, not a fully linked migration or vital-registration study. Legal boundary continuity has not been proved. Precinct totals must not be equated with the built-up village. The 1940 census has one unread age; sample questions have overwrites and omissions. Counts agreeing with published totals establish row coverage, not the correctness of every transcription.
