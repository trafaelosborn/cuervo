# Cattle prices and smaller households: follow-up

*September 19, 2026. Supplements the eight-page rebound brief; the PDF has not been revised for this follow-up.*

## New evidence: prices rose as well as volume

The earlier 7,399-to-19,724 comparison was the number of cattle/calves sold in Guadalupe County in 1939 and 1944. It was not a price comparison. A newly checked USDA price series now supplies separate price evidence.

Method on printed/PDF page 3. Both pages visually checked. [USDA original](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/usda-esmis/files/x920fw86z/5t34sp07q/df65vc27m/prflvstckdypltry_Prices_Received_by_Farmers_for_Beef_Cattle__1909-59.pdf).

Prices rose **56.5% in 1939-1944** and **209.7% in 1939-1949**. The annual average in 1949 was about **3.10 times** 1939. For the census years, the 1950 annual average was **3.16 times** 1940, although the full 1950 average includes months after the April population census. The already-completed 1949 year supplies the cleaner pre-enumeration observation.

These are statewide annual averages on a live-weight basis, excluding calves, with monthly prices weighted by estimated shares of annual sales. They are nominal dollars and are affected by the mix of cattle sold; they do not establish a tripling of real profits. Do not multiply them directly by the county cattle-and-calves head counts: product coverage and units differ and sale weights are unknown.

**Interpretation:** the economic explanation is more credible with independently observed rises in marketed head and cattle prices. The strongest price rise extends into the postwar years. A useful working hypothesis is that wartime and postwar cattle activity supported or attracted a few Cuervo households. The data still do not allocate Cuervo's 22-person gain among cattle demand, other employment, births, deaths, and moves.

## Household size: distinguish wartime absence from later living arrangements

Military departures could reduce particular parental households. But the comparison endpoint is April 1950, several years after World War II. A lasting effect could operate through casualties, continued service, settlement elsewhere, or returning veterans establishing separate households. Temporary wartime absence by itself is not a complete explanation for the 1950 snapshot.

**Draft-timing clarification:** the end of WWII did not end military conscription. The wartime act expired March 31, 1947, and a new act was enacted June 24, 1948. A contemporary account records inductions in November-December 1948 and January 1949, with a 21-month active-service term. Those entrants could therefore still be serving at the April 1, 1950 census. Active-service absence remains a viable explanation for particular missing household members; the earlier emphasis on postwar household formation was too narrow if read as excluding it. [Robert H. Rankin, "A History of Selective Service," October 1951](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1951/october/history-selective-service). The Korean War began June 25, 1950, after that census date, so the subsequent mobilization cannot cause an absence at the April reference date. [Army chronology](https://www.army.mil/koreanwar/?level=1); [NARA census dates](https://www.archives.gov/research/census/1850-1950). Conscription continued through 1973; [Selective Service's FY2003 report](https://www.sss.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/AnRepFY03.pdf) records the last draftee reporting June 30, 1973. These national facts establish a possible mechanism, not how many Cuervo residents were affected.

The existing transcription supplies a candidate example: Henry Yates, 24, appears in his parents' household in 1940; Henry Yates, 34, heads a separate three-person household in 1950 and answers Yes to WWII service. The family link is strong but not externally verified. The census does not say the war caused his move or marriage.

Population increased from 188 to 210 while head-listed groups increased from 42 to 59. More households can therefore explain the falling average without a falling total population. A few service-related absences should not be asserted as quantitatively sufficient for the whole change: as an illustration, five net departures with the 1940 count of 42 households held fixed would reduce the mean only from 4.48 to 4.36, compared with the observed 3.56 in 1950. That illustration is not an estimate of departures or a counterfactual population forecast.

**Revised working sentence:** Stronger cattle markets may have sustained modest population growth, while young adults leaving parental homes and forming new households helped reduce average household size; wartime service may have contributed to those life changes.
