# Cuervo railway functions: a 1958 passenger-service change, with freight and staffing dates still open

*September 20, 2026. Primary timetable and station-directory research. Supersedes the 1955 passenger-stop lead in the September 19 note.*

## Answer

**Cuervo still had advertised passenger stops in the Rock Island timetable effective April 27, 1958. The Southern Pacific operating timetable effective October 26, 1958 shows no regular or flag stop there. Those two snapshots bracket a change in scheduled passenger service within 1958.** They do not identify the exact last boarding date or establish the end of local freight handling.

Cuervo was also marked as a train-order office in September 1956, but that designation is absent in October 1958. A train-order office handled operating instructions for trains; its designation is not an employee roster or a freight-agency certificate. The passenger and office changes may be related, but their dates must not be treated as identical without another record.

**No final cattle-loading date, freight-agency closure order, or last station payroll has been located.** The 1967 station directory supplies an important reason to search Rock Island records: Southern Pacific had through-running rights on Rock Island-owned track between Santa Rosa and Tucumcari, with no local pickup or delivery under the SP freight listing. An SP blank is therefore not proof that Rock Island had stopped serving local customers.

**Later freight finding:** Rock Island's December 15, 1974 Station Book lists Cuervo with no agent, its own revenue/audit code 8355 and car-accounting code 16178, and billing code 8310, identifying Tucumcari. This establishes a published accounting arrangement after passenger service disappeared, but is not proof of a cattle shipment or usable stockyards. The carrier's Yard Clerical Manual also explicitly describes customers requesting cars. See the [1974 freight-accounting study](cuervo_freight_accounting_1974_2026-09-20.md) for verified pages, codes and more precise archival targets.

The `f` definition was checked directly in the Rock Island reference notes: a stop on signal or notice to the conductor. The October 1958 conditional-stop table contains no Cuervo exception; its “any station” provisions concern another part of the division.

The public schedules do not put the `n` (“No Agent”) symbol after Cuervo, although nearby Montoya and Newkirk carry it. This is a useful staffing lead, but a public timetable's missing symbol is insufficient to identify an employee, their employer, or their last workday. Do not turn it into a verified April 1958 payroll observation.

## Ownership, operations and local freight

The [January 26, 1965 ICC decision](https://wx4.org/to/foam/maps/1misc/docs/1965-01-26SP_39%2640ICCorder2continue.pdf), Finance Docket 23272, 328 ICC 14, PDF 3 / printed 16, explains the passenger arrangement. Rock Island owned the Santa Rosa–Tucumcari line and its passenger tariffs applied; Southern Pacific operated the trains as Rock Island's agent. Thus a train appearing in an SP timetable does not establish that SP alone controlled every local commercial function.

The [1967 SP directory](https://wx4.org/to/foam/maps/1misc/docs/1967-10-01SP_List_of_Agencies_Stations_Etc-Wx4.org.pdf), printed 98, states:

> No traffic pick-up or delivery—SP Co. has only bridge rights over CRI&P RR. tracks between Santa Rosa and Tucumcari.

That footnote applies to Cuervo and several neighboring operating points. Cuervo's classification is an asterisk, **not** the directory's category for a station without an agent or its category for a point where freight is not handled. Blank livestock and agency columns on this SP list therefore cannot settle the status of Rock Island facilities.

Santa Rosa is listed with livestock-corral capacity of 28 carloads and water, and Rock Island-owned stock scales. Tucumcari has capacity of 100 carloads and water. These are facility descriptions in 1967, not shipment totals, and do not prove that Cuervo traffic moved to either place.

Similarly, comparing Cuervo's 103-car siding designation in 1956–1959 with 92 in 1967 does not establish that track was shortened. The capacity basis and car lengths must be checked before using that difference physically.

## Follow-up: livestock shipments could continue without passenger stops or a resident agent

*Added September 20 in response to the question about ranchers arranging freight pickup.*

Advance ordering of livestock cars was an established practice. Charles S. Plumb's USDA Farmers' Bulletin 184, *Marketing Live Stock* (1903), printed page 33, section “Making a Shipment,” describes advance arrangements with the railway agent, obtaining cars from elsewhere if necessary, and switching them into position for loading. It says two or three days' notice was usually expected in a smaller town. This describes historical practice, not a verified Cuervo notice requirement. [Original bulletin, indexed text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9c/Marketing_live_stock_%28IA_CAT87201621%29.pdf).

A rancher, livestock buyer, or shipping association could arrange cars and a shipment; the railroad would place empties at a loading facility and arrange movement of the loaded cars. Pickup could be part of a freight train's regular work or a specifically arranged movement. Ordering cars did not inherently mean hiring a dedicated train, and the passenger timetable did not govern that transaction.

There is a particularly relevant documented example of the staffing distinction. *Thomson v. Nebraska State Railway Commission*, 141 Neb. 697 (July 3, 1942), printed page 702, describes a proposal to replace Nenzel's agent with a caretaker while neighboring agents handled bills of lading and livestock contracts through telephone arrangements. The case discusses car placement and a cattle-shipping facility separately from the resident agent. This is an example from another railroad and state, not evidence of Cuervo's arrangements. [Decision text](https://calculators.law/caselaw/decisions/X6ORVmLdNPMB/thomson-v-nebraska-state-railway-commission).

**Implication for Cuervo:** continued cattle loading after 1958 is operationally plausible. It would require a usable livestock-loading facility and track, freight service available from that point, and arrangements to supply and collect stock cars. A passing siding alone proves none of those. Conversely, ending passenger stops or removing the resident agent does not prove livestock service ended. Freight traffic could also have ended before the passenger stops; their sequence remains unestablished.

The decisive records are dated Cuervo livestock waybills and car orders, station freight/commodity returns, livestock tariff entries and supplements, and stockyard lease or retirement records. An actual post-1958 shipment would establish continued use, while an official availability listing would establish offered service; these are different observations. Preserve separate fields for passenger access, agency staffing, freight availability, physical loading facilities and actual shipments.

Direct page/PDF retrieval failed; original images were not inspected and the sources have not been downloaded. These passages support the general logistics explanation, not a new Cuervo shipment date.

## What this changes in the Cuervo argument

We can now place a **scheduled passenger-access loss within 1958**, after the documented 1952 road relocation and before the identified 1963–1965 interstate project. This is a dated service change worth testing against business, household and school evidence. It cannot explain the onset of population decline decades earlier, and it does not prove that highway changes or the cattle-price cycle caused the railway decision.

The cattle question remains especially valuable. We need to distinguish a ranching economy that kept shipping locally from one that retained cattle production while trucking animals to another loading point. Station shipment returns, stockyard leases/retirement records and freight tariffs would distinguish those possibilities better than the passenger timetable.

The defensible narrative is that **local access and operating functions could disappear while trains kept passing**. It is premature to date every loss to one event or call 1958 the end of “the railway.” The Golden State's 1968 withdrawal and the active through-freight corridor documented in the [September 19 report](rail_service_2026-09-19.md) remain separate facts.
