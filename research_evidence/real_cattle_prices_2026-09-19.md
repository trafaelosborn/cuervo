# Real cattle prices and Cuervo's secondary businesses

*September 19, 2026. A purchasing-power check and a testable business-history hypothesis; supplements the existing rebound brief.*

## Finding

New Mexico cattle prices had a large rise and subsequent collapse even after consumer-price inflation. The real annual price rose **80.9% from 1939 to 1949**, then fell **49.7% from 1951 to 1953** and **51.7% from 1951 to 1956**. That supplies a plausible economic shock to investigate alongside later Cuervo business losses. It does not yet establish that those losses occurred at the same time: the comparable Cuervo population observations currently assembled end in 1950, and a dated business-opening/closure series is still missing.

The later recovery is also useful. By 1973 the real cattle price was nearly twice its 1956 level and only 4.2% below its 1951 peak. If Cuervo's services failed to recover during that improvement, declining cattle prices alone would be an incomplete explanation. Local employment, how much ranch income stayed nearby, and where households bought goods would become central tests.

## Measurement and sources

These are **prices received by farmers for live beef cattle, excluding calves**, in nominal dollars per 100 pounds of liveweight. They are not retail beef prices. The New Mexico observations average the animals actually sold, so changes in grades, categories, and sales mix can change the average. They are statewide context, not a Cuervo price quote.

- **1913–1959:** USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, Statistical Bulletin 265, *Prices Received by Farmers for Beef Cattle, 1909–59* (May 1960), Table 43, printed/PDF page 46; method page 3. [Original USDA PDF](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/usda-esmis/files/x920fw86z/5t34sp07q/df65vc27m/prflvstckdypltry_Prices_Received_by_Farmers_for_Beef_Cattle__1909-59.pdf). Original held locally; annual column visually checked.
- **1959–1978:** USDA Statistical Reporting Service, Statistical Bulletin 680, *Prices Received by Farmers: Cattle, Milk Cows, Hogs, Sheep, Wool, by State and United States, 1959–78* (January 1982), beef-cattle table, New Mexico, printed/PDF page 16; methods pages 4–5. [Original USDA PDF](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/usda-esmis/files/x920fw86z/xg94hs65j/rv042x51f/prflvstckdypltry_Prices_Received_by_Farmers_-_Cattle__Milk_Cows__Hogs__Sheep__Wool__by_State_and_United_States__1959-78.pdf). Newly held locally, 159 pages; annual column visually checked. The publication explicitly continues bulletin 265, and the overlapping 1959 observation agrees at $22.30.
- **Inflation:** BLS, *Historical Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers: U.S. city average, all items, index averages*, December 2023 release, PDF pages 3–4, **Annual avg.** column; 1982–84=100. [BLS table](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/tables/supplemental-files/historical-cpi-u-202312.pdf). Annual values retrieved from the BLS document through web text and transcribed. Direct download returned HTTP 403; no locally held original or visual page check is claimed. In this table 1959 is **29.1**.
- **Population:** the [verified precinct observations and source locators](population_series_verified.md). Census units are not assumed to have constant boundaries. The later county-division counts cannot substitute for Cuervo precinct counts.

Calculation: **1950-dollar price = nominal price × 24.1 ÷ that year's annual CPI-U.** The 24.1 is the published 1950 CPI-U. This measures consumer purchasing power per unit sold, not profit margins or total ranch income. Feed, labor, freight, debt, herd size, and sale weights are not controlled. Consumer CPI is a broad deflator, not a historical Cuervo ranch-input index. Do not multiply this price directly by the county cattle-and-calves head counts: the units and product coverage differ.

### The earlier decades prevent a simple price-population story

At decade endpoints, 1920–1930 real prices fell 8.0% while population fell 34.5%; 1930–1940 real prices **rose 38.2%** while population fell another 34.5%. Yet those endpoints conceal a substantial Depression slump: real prices fell 36.7% between 1930 and 1934 before recovering. Business closures or departures during a slump need not reverse as soon as prices recover. Sparse census snapshots cannot locate those changes within a decade.

For 1940–1950, real prices rose 83.8% and population rose 11.7%. This is consistent with stronger ranching conditions supporting the small rebound, alongside the independent county sales-volume evidence. It neither identifies the cause of the 22-person gain nor establishes that census-year price levels explain population. No correlation coefficient or fitted population model is warranted from these few observations.

## Secondary businesses: existing evidence and missing evidence

The strongest question is whether Cuervo's services expanded and survived the cattle cycle. Existing records already show commerce:

- The **1920 and 1921 directories** list hotels, merchants, a garage, and other services. Named 1920 observations include Armijo Mercantile, Bond & Wiest, Golden Rule Garage, Oklahoma Hotel, and Rock Island Hotel. These are dated listings, not exact opening/closure dates. [Verified directory readings](additional_acquisitions_2026-09-18.md).
- The **1950 resident occupation readings** include six retail descriptions, a café manager, café waitress, bartender, service-station work, and a beautician. One retail classification is provisional. These are resident occupations across the larger precinct; they do **not** establish the number or location of storefronts or when a business opened. [Row-level reading log](deep_dive_2026-09-17/cuervo_1950_reading_log.md).
- A complete comparable 1940 occupation inventory and a dated 1930s–1970s business series remain unfinished. The held Clipper issues end in 1922 and cannot directly document a 1940s business boom. The 1946 Rittenhouse guide entry has not yet been directly verified.

## Distinguishing the explanations

| Hypothesis | Evidence that would strengthen it |
|---|---|
| A cattle bust reduced local purchasing power | Dated business contraction and household departures following the early-1950s price fall, with local cattle sales/income or employment also weakening. |
| Ranching income increasingly supported businesses in larger towns | Cuervo service losses during healthy/recovering cattle conditions, together with merchant accounts, shopping testimony, freight/delivery records, or other direct evidence locating spending elsewhere. |
| The boom added income without many local workers | Ranch records showing more output or receipts with little hiring, or income concentrated among owners living/spending elsewhere. Price increases alone do not establish that pattern. |
| Changed road access reduced customers | Business-specific evidence around the documented 1952 relocation and, separately, the interstate project. The early-1950s price collapse and road relocation overlap, so timing alone cannot separate them. |

**Working mechanism to test:** a downturn closes a store; residents begin shopping elsewhere; a later cattle recovery increases their spending at the surviving regional center, without restoring the Cuervo store. This can connect commodity cycles with the larger-town hypothesis, but it is currently a proposed mechanism, not an observed sequence. Business dates, employment, and actual customers' destinations are the missing links.

The most useful next additions are dated establishment observations around **1940, 1950, 1953–1956, 1960, and the 1960s/early-1970s recovery**, plus enrollment/household evidence where comparable population counts are unavailable. Each observation should identify business, proprietor, site, source date, and whether the source explicitly states an opening/closure or merely lists an operating concern. Compare the same services in Santa Rosa and other nearby communities exposed to the cattle cycle.
