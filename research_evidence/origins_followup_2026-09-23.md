# Why the Cuervo business opened: a new source and a railway correction

September 23, 2026. A focused follow-up to the origins question. This report separates Grubbs's reconstruction, contemporary newspaper observations, and our interpretation.

## What we acquired and read

**Frank H. Grubbs's 1958 UNM MBA thesis is now held in full:** *Frank Bond: Gentleman Sheepherder of Northern New Mexico, 1883–1915*. [Repository record](https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/anderson_etds/11/); [local source PDF](../sources/origins_followup_2026-09-23/grubbs_bond_thesis_1958.pdf).

The download is 430,996,011 bytes and 628 PDF pages, including covers, blank versos and plates. It is not 628 pages of continuous text. The body is image-only. The dedicated **Bond & Wiest chapter is printed pp. 96–115**, at physical PDF pp. 227–269, with blank/plate pages interspersed. Selected-page OCR and renders are preserved in the companion folder; this is not a claim to have read or OCRed the whole thesis.

Grubbs consulted company accounts and correspondence and interviewed J. S. Holbrook at Cuervo on March 1, 1958. His footnotes distinguish records then at Cuervo, which he calls the Holbrook Papers, from other Bond records. We have acquired **his thesis**, not those original manuscripts. It is unusually useful research, but the railway claim discussed below shows why its interpretations require independent checks.

The public PDF opened and downloaded through the ordinary UNM browser link after direct requests returned HTTP 403. The [manifest](../sources/origins_followup_2026-09-23/download_manifest.json) records the held PDF and checksum. The separately published journal installment was not acquired; it is not counted as a holding.

| Cuervo building completed in 1903 and warehouse added that year; corporation formed in 1904, with first stockholders' meeting dated April 21 | 101 / 237 | Building, business relocation, charter date and first corporate meeting are separate events |
| Company provided merchandise and credit, bought wool, managed sheep interests, and combined hides shipments | 105–111 / 249–261 | These are multiple functions of one firm; they cannot automatically be assigned to every merchant |
| J. S. Holbrook still operated the Cuervo business when Grubbs wrote | 115 / 269 | A contemporary 1958 survival observation; not a closure date or proof of unchanged ownership |

Grubbs interprets the original branch as a way to serve and supervise an expanding sheep economy and retain trade as new railway routes changed access. He attributes the relocation to Cuervo to rail access. His discussion of why Cabra was first selected includes acknowledged uncertainty and retrospective testimony. We should seek the actual investment letters before treating the inferred strategy as the owners' stated original plan.

**Our implication:** a business could locate here to connect dispersed producers, credit, goods and distant buyers. The customer base need not have been restricted to people living beside the store. This gives us a specific mechanism to investigate behind the previously vague idea of a ranch-service settlement.

Business-line figures are **not additive components reconciled to net profit**: omitted activities, expenses and accounting treatment still need investigation.

The complete net-profit table supplies twelve positive annual observations, 1904–1915. It records a trough in 1910–1911 followed by recovery through 1915. Merchandise gross profit also recovers after 1912. That is not a steadily falling series, even though Grubbs's prose describes a continuing commercial decline after 1910. These numbers do not establish real growth, comparable modern accounting profit, or prosperity across the whole settlement.

Sales, as reported in his prose, rose from $44,230.32 in 1905 to almost $96,000 in 1908, fell below $61,000 in 1912, and returned to about $79,000 in 1915. Preserve the approximations rather than manufacturing exact annual observations or filling the missing years.

**What changes in our research:** we can begin examining actual business performance instead of relying entirely on population and cattle prices. Sheep, wool, merchandise, receivables and freight arrangements deserve separate attention. This firm's early business mix does not demonstrate that all surrounding ranching was sheep-based or determine when cattle became more important locally.

## A railway claim that fails an independent check

Grubbs reports loss of regular stops by late 1904, continued flag service, and then an end to stopping after December 1, 1910. His last claim cites a December 23, 1910 copy-book entry. He distinguishes passenger service from continuing sheep and wool business, but his broad account of permanent passenger loss cannot stand as written.

Two held Clipper issues supply contemporary contrary observations:

1. **December 9, 1910, front page/PDF 2, repository page 2691:** a local item reports visitors taking the train from Cuervo the preceding Monday. A separate time card lists No. 33 westbound at 9:30 a.m. and No. 34 eastbound at 5:47 p.m.
2. **January 20, 1911, front page/PDF 2, repository page 2713:** an item reports J. J. Davis and J. K. Dobbins getting off the train, Boyd Ponder arriving on the same train, and two cattle buyers arriving from Amarillo. The time card remains present.

The narrative boarding/alighting reports matter more than a time card alone, which might conceivably have been stale. Both full pages and the relevant article crops were visually checked. [Page locators](origins_followup_2026-09-23/clipper_selected_pages.json); original [December issue](../sources/02_newspapers/cuervo_clipper/full_scans/The%20Cuervo%20Clipper,%2012-09-1910%20-%20The%20Cuervo%20Clipper%2012-09-1910.pdf) and [January issue](../sources/02_newspapers/cuervo_clipper/full_scans/The%20Cuervo%20Clipper,%2001-20-1911%20-%20The%20Cuervo%20Clipper%2001-20-1911.pdf).

Our previously read later timetables independently show advertised passenger stops through April 1958; see the [railway study](rail_local_functions_2026-09-20.md). The new findings do not identify the exact service affected in 1910. A short interruption, a particular train, a restriction on stopping, or a misreading remains possible. Obtain the cited letter and contemporary schedules before selecting an explanation. The 1958 bracket remains a separately documented change and should not be overwritten by the thesis.

## Two additional primary observations from July 1910

The **July 15, 1910 Clipper, local PDF 5, repository page 2601**, reports wool arriving from the surrounding country and Bond & Wiest receiving separate cars of grain, flour and fruit. Its praise of the firm's prices is promotional; it does not establish a monopoly, precise catchment or transaction volume. It nevertheless supports the two-way flow of rural produce and incoming supplies. [Original issue](../sources/02_newspapers/cuervo_clipper/full_scans/The%20Cuervo%20Clipper,%2007-15-1910%20-%20The%20Cuervo%20Clipper%2007-15-1910.pdf).

On the same page, a notice says **Dr. J. C. Woodburn chose Cuervo after considering locations elsewhere in New Mexico and arranged to take charge of the existing drug business**. This is another opening/transfer decision we can investigate by following a named person. It does not identify his expected patient income, demonstrate profitability, or mean medicine had previously been absent. The paper's confidence in his prospects is not a financial result.

The December 9 issue also juxtaposes a visitor's doubts about farming with the editor's optimism about settlement and criticism of established stockmen. This is evidence of competing expectations, not a reliable test of agricultural capacity or a measured account of ranchers' motives. Its reported 1886 arrival of Mr. Ray is a useful early-settlement lead, explicitly presented as hearsay in the paper.

## What we can do next without a visit

### 1. Test changes in the sheep economy

Grubbs's grazing discussion cites Copy Book No. 635, January 27, 1909, p. 487, then follows with a warning about homesteaders taking grazing land under an abbreviated “Ibid.” citation; the original should settle that warning's exact date and context. He also cites an August 11, 1915 letter about sheep prospects and possible sale of the Cuervo business (pp. 109–110 and 114). These are focused leads. His own account records over 11,000 company sheep in 1915, including 8,800 on rent. A gloomy forecast is not proof of disappearance.

I can compare the held agricultural census material, relevant public land maps and newspaper reports for changes in sheep, cattle, land use and labor. County statistics will be labeled as county evidence; they cannot substitute for Cuervo ranch accounts. The question is whether the local business opportunities changed even while livestock operations persisted.

### 2. Reconstruct specific businesses and their customers

The held newspaper corpus can be searched systematically for named proprietors, wool buyers, suppliers, arrivals, shipments, transfers and explicit opening/closing decisions. This pass created 703 OCR leads across four broad patterns; most have not been individually read and are not 703 verified events. The next useful unit of work is a named firm or proprietor, followed across dates and checked against the page images.

Other chapters of the newly held thesis also offer comparisons with Wagon Mound, Encino and other Bond branches. They can help distinguish a shared company practice from something particular to Cuervo. The rest of the thesis remains available for targeted reading.

### 3. Narrow the transport and transaction questions

The exact 1905, 1907, 1910 and 1915 references now let us connect merchant decisions to particular freight arrangements and passenger services. Further publicly accessible timetable and newspaper research can test those links. The 1906 *American Shepherd's Bulletin* visit cited by Grubbs, p. 100 n. 12, is another independent contemporary target; this pass did not retrieve it.

## What still needs original records

The ledgers, customer addresses, original business letters, incorporation minutes, land titles and town-company book remain important. They let us audit a researcher's summary, follow individual transactions, resolve the railway contradiction and move beyond a profitable firm's aggregate results.

The [updated UNM pull list](unm_origins_pull_list_2026-09-23.md) now cross-references Grubbs's specific letters to candidate present-day containers. **Volume 84, spine 59, August 11, 1915, p. 384** is especially promising for the sale discussion and business prospects. **Volume 58, copy book November 1910–August 1913**, is a date-range candidate for the disputed railway letter; the catalog does not itself prove identity.

The defensible current proposition is that **Cuervo hosted useful, sometimes profitable connections between producers and wider markets, and those connections could change independently of whether ranching survived**. We now have a much stronger route to testing that proposition. We still do not have a complete causal account of its eventual commercial contraction.
