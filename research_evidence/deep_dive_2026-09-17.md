# Cuervo and Santa Rosa: what the new evidence changes

**September 18 addendum:** the [new census analysis](census_deep_dive_2026-09-18.md) extends the comparison through 2020 and supplies Cuervo's complete 1950 age profile. It also corrects a transcription below: Santa Rosa's 1970 population is **2,485**, not 2,483; the 1960-70 increase is **265 / 11.9%**. The 1970 county remainder is **2,484**, a decline of **906 / 26.7%**. The September 17 prose, PDF, and derived files retain the original reading as a dated research record; use the September 18 report and current population note for the corrected values and interpretation of later growth.

## Research assessment | September 17, 2026

**The strongest explanation now is a sequence of changes in a region whose towns already had different roles.** Santa Rosa had government, distribution, finance, and railway functions well before Route 66. Cuervo experienced substantial precinct population loss before the interstate, yet its 1950 residents still worked in ranching, railroading, commerce, education, and public services. The interstate's additional effect remains an open question. Neither a single catastrophic bypass story nor a single inexorable transfer of everything to Santa Rosa adequately describes the evidence.

This report combines a close reading of the new acquisitions with selected sources already held. It includes a manual count of the named persons and main occupation entries on **all nine Cuervo 1950 population sheets**, the two 1908 Santa Rosa Sanborn sheets, the 1929 retail tables and relevant definitions, the 1902 railway report passage, the January 1926 local timetable, the December 1, 1922 Clipper editorial, published population tables, and the substantive highway-nomination narratives. It is **not** an exhaustive reading of the 267 newspaper PDFs or a transcription of the other five 1950 census districts. Source numbers below point to exact files and page locators in Section 12.

### The six findings that matter most

1. **A Cuervo newspaper investor advocated consolidation at the county seat in 1922.** Katharine A. Hicks said the county could not support more than one first-class weekly under existing economic conditions. This is contemporary evidence of a perceived market-size problem, before Route 66's designation. Her political motives and ownership connections also matter. [S1]
2. **Santa Rosa was already a disproportionate retail center in 1929.** It had 16.0% of Guadalupe County's population, 34.3% of its retail establishments, and 44.5% of reported retail sales. [S2]
3. **Cuervo was still a working community in 1950.** Its census has 210 named persons and 56 entries describing current or last occupations. Half of those descriptions concern ranch work; the rest include substantial railway and service activity. These are residents' occupations, not a count of Cuervo businesses. [S5]
4. **Rail access favored different trips in different directions.** January 1926's published Cuervo stops allowed a daytime visit to Tucumcari and an evening return. The Santa Rosa service did not offer that pattern. [S6]
5. **The 1952 highway relocation must be investigated separately from I-40.** The southwest Route 66 alignment had already been superseded before the documented 1963-65 interstate project. [S9-S10]
6. **Santa Rosa's 1960-70 growth occurred inside a shrinking county.** The municipality gained 263 residents while the rest of the county lost 904. That is geographic concentration in the counts, not proof that those particular people moved to Santa Rosa. [S8]

## 1. The Clipper: a contemporary argument for consolidation

### What Hicks actually said

The December 1, 1922 front page carries **“To My Clipper Readers,” signed Katharine A. Hicks**. She announces withdrawal from all financial connection with the Cuervo paper. Her principal stated economic argument is unusually explicit:

> “under present economic conditions it is not possible for the people of Guadalupe County to support more than one first class weekly paper, and I believe that one should be published at the County seat.”

She proposes a county newspaper with allotted space for each town or neighborhood, including local happenings and advertisements. She argues that this would serve residents and the supporting merchants more fairly than several small papers. Crucially, she does not present this as an eternal rule: she says the conclusion might change when the country became more thickly settled. [S1, front page, columns 4-5]

This is valuable because it identifies an economic mechanism in a participant's own words: **a limited pool of readers and advertisers, divided among publications with competing costs**. It also identifies an institutional location for consolidation: the county seat. We no longer have to infer that every idea of regional concentration arrived with the paved highway.

### The complications make it better evidence

Hicks was an interested participant. Her associate, Charles Gerhardt, already owned and published a Democratic paper in Santa Rosa, identified later in the article as the **Public Voice**. She endorsed his enlargement plans and intended to continue contributing. She also explained that the Hickses had purchased the Clipper to return it to Democratic alignment and help elect Democratic county and state tickets. Those objectives had been achieved; they were treating their considerable financial loss as a campaign contribution.

That means the article supports several explanations at once: a perceived insufficient market, financial loss, a political objective that had run its course, and a planned reorganization involving an associate. It is not an independent audit proving that only one county newspaper could survive. The teaching contract mentioned belongs to **Gerhardt**. This editorial does not supply a husband's-health explanation for Hicks's decision.

Nor does it establish an exact closure date. Withdrawal by an investor, transfer of responsibility, the last surviving issue, and cessation of publication are different events. The safest description is **an announced financial withdrawal and a proposal to concentrate support**, dated December 1, 1922.

### Read the advertisements beside the argument

The same front page contains an Armijo Mercantile advertisement explicitly located in Cuervo and a Bond-West advertisement explicitly located in Cuervo, urging readers to **“BUY AT HOME.”** A Santa Rosa Mercantile Co. advertisement seeks corn, maize, eggs, and beans. The company name alone does not establish the location of the advertised outlet.

The juxtaposition is revealing: a case for regional newspaper consolidation appears beside appeals to sustain local merchandise trade. Different activities could have different viable markets. Newspaper consolidation did not mean that every Cuervo service had ceased to function. The grain-and-produce advertisement also directs attention to merchants as intermediaries between the agricultural hinterland and outside markets, rather than only as suppliers to highway travelers.

**Effect on the thesis:** strong support for pre-interstate, and even pre-designation, pressure toward concentration in one documented sector. No demonstrated transfer of all commercial functions, no measured effect of road speed, and no automatic newspaper death date.

The newly downloaded retail census gives us a quantitative economic observation before the dramatic 1930s population divergence. The census is called “1930,” but the business activity measured is **1929**. Its population denominator is 1930. Dollar figures below are nominal dollars converted from printed thousands. [S2-S4]

**Interpretation:** Santa Rosa's commercial role was substantially larger than its population share. This is consistent with serving a wider market. It is not evidence that 44.5% of Cuervo's spending went there, nor that the difference came entirely from travelers. The county remainder includes other towns, including Vaughn, as well as farms and ranches.

Sales per resident were approximately **$558 in Santa Rosa versus $133 in the rest of the county**, a ratio of about 4.2. This is a descriptive measure of business concentration. It is not household income, residents' consumption, or a direct estimate of money drawn from other towns. Two details deserve attention:

- **General stores supplied 50.2% of Santa Rosa's reported retail sales.** The census definition includes country general stores combining groceries with other merchandise. An absence of a separately classified clothing or hardware store does not mean those goods were unavailable. [S4, printed p. 4]
- **Santa Rosa's five businesses classified as filling stations reported $33,000 of the county's $38,000 in that category, or 86.8%.** This is striking, but it is not an estimate of the town's share of all fuel sold: general stores, garages, and other classifications could also sell gasoline. Nor does the table distinguish local customers from through motorists. Filling-station-classified receipts were just 5.2% of Santa Rosa's total retail receipts; the town's commercial position cannot be reduced to those stations.

The census's full-time employee counts were based on averages of reported employment dates where possible, but sometimes only a count at the enumerator's visit was available. Owners off payroll are separate. These are not modern full-time-equivalent labor inputs. Dividing sales or payroll by 48 cannot establish productivity or average annual wages. Store coverage could also miss businesses discontinued during 1929 or shortly afterward. [S4, printed pp. 12-13]

### Tucumcari belongs in the comparison

The same table records **88 retail establishments and $3.038 million in sales in Tucumcari**, with 4,143 residents. It accounted for about 76.6% of Quay County's retail sales with 38.3% of its population. Its reported sales were about 4.8 times Santa Rosa's. [S2]

This supports treating Cuervo as situated among several unequal centers. It does not identify where Cuervo households actually shopped. The timetable supplies a separate reason to investigate eastward connections.

## 3. Cuervo in 1950: a small community with several economic supports

### Population count: 210 visible named persons

The Cuervo enumeration district, **10-6**, corresponds to Election Precinct 6. It extends beyond the built-up settlement. I counted named person rows across every retained population sheet, excluding vacant-property and absent-household placeholders and including the four callback entries on sheet 71. [S5]

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

That corroborates the published precinct count and the portfolio's field total of 210. The cover also records **211 in the Washington-office total and 1 under population transcribed from ICRs**. That suggests an administrative lead, not a resolved explanation. The visible schedule count does not identify how that extra record was treated in the published tabulation.

Sheet 71 is an out-of-order/callback sheet, not evidence that sheets 9-70 are missing. NARA explains this numbering convention. The cover's agricultural-form tally does not mean we possess those farm schedules; NARA states that the 1950 agricultural schedules were destroyed. [S13]

At least one other person has a looking-for-work response without a main occupation description; consequently this 56-entry inventory must not be used to calculate an unemployment rate. [S5; complete sheet-and-line log accompanies this report]

Important locators include the teachers at sheet 4, lines 6 and 8; railway agent at 4:17; postmaster at 5:2; service-station role at 5:14; cafe manager at 5:15; bartender at 5:21; mail carrier at 5:23; cafe waitress at 6:3; beautician at 7:5; and veterans instructor at 8:21. The government worker at 3:3 is a **livestock inspector**, not a school janitor.

### What this changes

**Cuervo cannot be described as already devoid of functioning social and economic life before I-40.** Its precinct's resident population supported or participated in several types of work in 1950. Ranching and the railway matter alongside roadside trade. Government-class education, inspection, and postal work point to income sources that were not simply motorists purchasing gasoline.

But the schedules record where people lived, not an exact workplace address. Two cafe-related residents do not establish two cafes, or even establish that either cafe was inside the built-up town. The same applies to teachers and stores. Linking these people to employers, parcels, advertisements, and school records is the next evidentiary step.

The cafe entries also make the older project's unverified characterization of the 1946 Rittenhouse guide especially important to check. If that guide truly omitted a cafe, several explanations remain possible: a later opening, selective guide coverage, or work outside Cuervo. A 1950 occupation cannot resolve a 1946 business inventory. We should retire the current assumption that service loss proceeded in a known, irreversible order.

The 1950 census is particularly valuable as a **baseline before the 1952 road relocation**. It should be linked to 1950s business directories, tax assessments, and school enrollment before jumping directly to the interstate years.

### Questions this transcription does not answer

No complete age, birthplace, household, income, or migration dataset has been created here. Some income and previous-residence questions were asked only on sample lines. A birthplace is not a migration date, and one year's previous residence does not reconstruct a lifetime movement history. The Santa Rosa and Newkirk schedules are acquired but have not received equivalent row-level coding. A comparative occupation percentage would therefore be premature. [S13]

## 4. The 1926 timetable: connection was directional

Table 2 on printed/PDF page 18 lists daily trains and Mountain Time. Cuervo is 17 timetable miles from Santa Rosa and 42 from Tucumcari. Those are railway mileages, not road distances. Its unqualified scheduled stops in the table are on trains 1 and 2. [S6]

| Journey | Train | Departure | Arrival | Scheduled duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cuervo to Santa Rosa | 1, westbound | 9:00 p.m. | 9:35 p.m. | 35 minutes |
| Santa Rosa to Cuervo | 2, eastbound | 6:20 a.m. | 7:00 a.m. | 40 minutes |
| Cuervo to Tucumcari | 2, eastbound | 7:00 a.m. | 8:20 a.m. | 80 minutes |
| Tucumcari to Cuervo | 1, westbound | 7:45 p.m. | 9:00 p.m. | 75 minutes |

The published service offered **11 hours 25 minutes between arrival in Tucumcari and the return departure**. The Santa Rosa pairing offered an overnight stay, not a daytime shopping or courthouse visit with a same-evening return. The other through trains shown at Santa Rosa lack Cuervo times; their stopping rules on page 19 should not be converted into an invented local service.

This answers the narrow question left open in the register: **this timetable does not demonstrate a Cuervo-Santa Rosa daytime day-return; it does demonstrate a Cuervo-Tucumcari day-return opportunity.** It does not prove actual shopping behavior, affordability, punctuality, service in other years, or the absence of any other transport option.

The larger implication is that distance alone is an incomplete measure of access. A nearby destination served at inconvenient hours can be less useful for a day trip than a farther one with a morning outward train and evening return. Automobile access could have changed the relative convenience of Santa Rosa without creating Cuervo's first access to any outside market. That is a testable hypothesis, not yet a demonstrated causal sequence.

## 5. Santa Rosa's advantages preceded the automobile highway

### The railway report

The June 7, 1902 Commercial and Financial Chronicle prints the Chicago Rock Island & Pacific annual report for the year ending March 31. Its “El Paso Extension” passage describes the line through subsidiary companies to Santa Rosa, where it connected with the El Paso & Rock Island Railway, part of the El Paso & Northeastern system. Agreements furnished through passenger and freight connections at El Paso to the Southern Pacific and Mexican Central. [S7]

The precise conclusion is that Santa Rosa was a **connection point between railway systems**. The passage does not establish a three-direction physical junction, the local railroad payroll, or Cuervo's station-opening date. Those distinctions matter if we are going to explain a town's economy through railway functions rather than simply through the presence of tracks.

### What the December 1908 Sanborn maps show

| Observation | Locator | Historical use |
|---|---|---|
| Railway station and nearby hotel | Sheet 1, upper edge | Passenger handling and accommodation before Route 66 |
| Chas. Ilfeld Co. wholesale warehouse for groceries and farm implements, beside a spur | Sheet 1, upper-left inset | Physical evidence of distribution connected to the railway |
| Bank and general stores | Sheet 1, block 4, Fourth/Parker area | Finance and merchandise functions |
| Warehouses, storage, cold storage, general merchandise | Sheet 1, block 74 | Commercial infrastructure beyond individual shopfronts |
| Lumber company premises | Sheet 1, block 3 | Building-material distribution |
| Restaurant, saloon, livery, printing premises, additional hotel labels | Sheet 1, blocks 28-29 and other mapped locations | Several distinct service functions |
| County courthouse | Sheet 2, block 35 | A public institution with a county-wide purpose |
| School | Sheet 2, block 36 | Educational infrastructure |
| M.E. Church; Catholic church marked “Work Suspended” | Sheet 2, block 59 | Institutional presence, with construction status preserved |

These maps replace a vague claim that Santa Rosa was “bigger” with specific early functions. The Ilfeld warehouse is especially useful: the town's relationship with its hinterland included supplying groceries and farm implements through a rail-connected distribution business. That could support activity that a town relying on occasional roadside stops would not share. The maps do not measure those businesses' sales or regional customer base. [S11]

They also complicate an overly smooth story of inevitable success. The title gives an estimated population of **700**, not a census count; the note states there was **no public water supply, no fire department or apparatus, unpaved streets, and no public lights**. A church construction project was suspended. Santa Rosa had significant institutions alongside limited public infrastructure. “No public water supply” does not mean no available water or disprove a later water advantage.

A mapped use is not a verified operating business census. The town's four “Hotel” labels cannot be compared directly with a guidebook's selected accommodations decades later. Nor can the absence of a located Cuervo Sanborn map be used to infer that Cuervo lacked comparable buildings.

Do not silently choose the number that fits the argument. This changes the municipality's calculated 1920-30 growth from 12.4% to 3.1%. The precinct's near-flat 1920-30 total is unaffected. [S12]

The earlier 1910 Cuervo precinct figure is 545, but the Census Bureau withheld a comparable 1910 precinct column in its 1920 table because of redistricting. Guadalupe County also lost territory in the creation of De Baca County in 1917. We cannot treat a 1910-40 percentage as a measured decline of the same town footprint. Nor can we say what proportion of the county's 1910-20 loss was administrative without a boundary reconstruction.

![Three separate population series, 1930-1950](deep_dive_2026-09-17/population_1930_1950.png)

### The 1930s divergence is real in the published units

- Cuervo precinct: **287 to 188**, a loss of 99, or 34.5%.
- Santa Rosa municipality: **1,127 to 2,310**, a gain of 1,183, or 105.0%.
- Guadalupe County: **7,027 to 8,646**, a gain of 1,619, or 23.0%.
- The county outside Santa Rosa: **5,900 to 6,336**, a gain of 436.

The last line matters. This was not a decade in which the entire remainder of the county simply emptied into Santa Rosa. Even a hypothetical transfer equal to Cuervo's net loss of 99 would account for only 8.4% of Santa Rosa's net gain. That arithmetic is **not an upper bound on actual migration**: births, deaths, inflows, and outflows can offset each other. It demonstrates why net totals cannot identify individual movement or explain the town's growth by themselves.

The evidence supports investigating the 1930s as an important interval. It cannot distinguish a sudden break after the 1936 paving from gradual change across the decade. Three decennial observations do not identify an economic “tipping point.” Railway changes, government employment, construction, household formation, regional migration, and road trade all remain possible contributors.

### The 1940s prevent a simple one-way story

Cuervo precinct then increased **11.7%**, while Santa Rosa municipality fell **4.8%** and the county fell **21.7%**. The new 1950 occupational observations show what remained, but do not explain the increase. We should not assign it to highway maintenance without evidence; the newly read occupation descriptions do not directly establish that proposed explanation.

The 1950 Guadalupe footnote has also been mischaracterized in earlier project prose. Its 1948 and 1945 annexations are mergers among **other county precincts**, not described as Santa Rosa municipal annexations. The table does not flag a Cuervo/Santa Rosa precinct change for 1930-50. That makes it the best presently held comparison, while not replacing a legal boundary history. [S12]

### The 1960s: growth and concentration are different measures

![Santa Rosa municipality and the remainder of Guadalupe County, 1960 and 1970](deep_dive_2026-09-17/population_1960_1970.png)

Santa Rosa municipality grew from **2,220 to 2,483**, or 11.8%. Guadalupe County declined from **5,610 to 4,969**, or 11.4%. Subtraction puts the county outside Santa Rosa at **3,390 to 2,486**, down 26.7%. Santa Rosa's share of county population rose from 39.6% to approximately 50.0%. [S8]

The larger Santa Rosa census county division declined from **3,380 to 3,221**. Its population outside the municipality therefore fell from **1,160 to 738**, or 36.4%, on the units reported in Table 10. The Guadalupe footnote records changes in municipal designation, not an annexation. These divisions still must not be spliced into the earlier precinct series or used as a Cuervo count.

Meanwhile, **Tucumcari city fell from 8,143 to 7,189**, or 11.7%, in 1960-70. Quay's footnote flags boundary changes, so this is a reported municipal comparison, not a fixed-footprint estimate. A town can remain an important center while losing population. The older “two thrived, three died” classification hides this distinction.

## 7. The road changed more than once

| Event | What the held evidence supports | What it does not settle |
|---|---|---|
| 1932 southwest realignment | NRHP narrative dates the Cuervo-SR 156 alignment to FAP 114-C; cites plans dated December 1, 1932 | Parcel-level changes in Cuervo, business losses, traffic shifts |
| 1935 plans / 1936 hard surfacing east of Cuervo | Montoya-Cuervo nomination dates hard surfacing to 1936 and cites plans of August 28, 1935 | An exact year of economic divergence or a causal effect size |
| 1951 improvements east of Cuervo | Additional culverts and roadbed stabilization described in the nomination | Which specific establishments benefited or were displaced |
| 1952 southwest relocation | Older road superseded by the course later followed by I-40 | Full local access arrangement, closures, takings, and construction sequence |
| 1963-65 interstate project | NTSB dates project I-040-5(18)1283, approximately MP 282.7-290.7 | Automatically dating every part of the Exit 291 interchange and adjacent project |

The 1993 nomination describes the southwest road climbing to Mesita Contadero and notes remains of a gas station at the rise. That creates a specific site to investigate for exposure to the 1952 relocation. The nomination does not date that station's closure. The 1997 Montoya-Cuervo account describes vacant garages, tourist courts, and cafes at Newkirk, which is evidence of visible former roadside functions at the survey date, not their closure chronology. [S9]

A highway can remain accessible by an exit while sharply changing the likelihood that a driver sees, enters, or returns to a particular business. Presence of an interchange alone does not establish equal access conditions. Conversely, a route moving away from one business does not prove that it caused the community's entire demographic history.

The NTSB's 1993-2001 traffic table is explicitly a count of eastbound and westbound **I-40 lanes in the accident vicinity**. Its “Cuervo Interchange” heading does not turn those mainline volumes into counts of vehicles exiting to patronize Cuervo. It also postdates the construction period by decades. The old summary's use of those volumes to dismiss a bypass effect was therefore unsound. [S10]

The acquired maps must be dated by their compilation notes, not only filenames. The county ED map uses a **1938 road base annotated for the 1950 census**. The Santa Fe regional sheets have printing/revision dates distinct from their road information; the Newkirk sheet has 1963 photography and 1964 field checking. A cartographic highway symbol on the 1963 Cuervo sheet is not by itself an opening certificate. The 1984 Santa Rosa quadrangle does not cover Cuervo town. [S5, S14]

The seven acquired Lucas photographs remain useful visual records, but their catalog dating is broad and the downloaded access images are small. They cannot yet date the closure of a particular business or prove conditions immediately before or after a specific highway project. No new closure dates are inferred from them here.

## 8. An explanation that fits the evidence better

### Phase A: unequal regional roles before Route 66

By 1902-08, Santa Rosa had documented railway connections, wholesale premises, finance, government, and other services. Cuervo's origins and its early business composition still need direct documentation. The two places did not begin as otherwise identical towns exposed to an identical highway treatment.

### Phase B: early contraction and selective consolidation

The precinct series shows Cuervo at 438 in 1920 and 287 in 1930. Hicks's 1922 statement documents pressure to consolidate newspaper support. Agricultural difficulty is a plausible rival or interacting explanation for population loss, but we have not yet measured local farm exits, production, rainfall, or credit conditions. The timetable shows that rail access could orient some day trips eastward. There is no basis yet to assign all early loss to Santa Rosa.

### Phase C: a larger commercial center grows during the 1930s

Santa Rosa's retail concentration is already visible in 1929; its municipal population then more than doubles. Paving occurs within that interval. The hypothesis is that improving roads enlarged the market reachable from an established center, while also creating or sustaining roadside trade elsewhere. Proving this requires annual business and household evidence, not just two endpoints.

### Phase D: persistence before another access change

Cuervo's 1950 precinct population and occupations show survival through several economic supports. This is the key counterweight to any account in which the town was already economically finished. The 1952 relocation and later interstate may have affected these supports differently. Roadside services, railroad workers, ranch households, and public-service workers should be tracked separately.

### Phase E: later concentration, with the mechanism still to establish

Santa Rosa's municipal growth in the 1960s occurred alongside loss in its surrounding division and county. That pattern is compatible with concentration of residents and services, but it does not identify the source of the city's residents or establish a highway effect. Business relocation, school consolidation, railway employment changes, demographic turnover, and wider rural decline need their own records.

**Working thesis:** Cuervo's decline unfolded through several changes in regional demand, institutions, employment, and access. Santa Rosa entered the highway era with a different set of functions. Roads may have amplified that difference and imposed additional losses on particular Cuervo businesses. The magnitude and timing of those effects remain to be demonstrated.

Economic geography remains useful for asking questions, but a theory should not substitute for a measured sequence of events. We have considerably better evidence for different regional roles and selective consolidation than for a single verified mechanism governing the entire town's fate.

## 10. What would most efficiently settle the remaining questions

### Highest-value tests

1. **Build the Clipper business and institution chronology.** Start with the named merchants, newspaper ownership changes, train/service notices, credit and crop reports, road petitions, and school news. Record first and last observed advertisements separately from actual opening and closure announcements. Missing issues make absence particularly weak evidence.
2. **Link the 1950 resident roles to places and employers.** Start with the station agent, service-station occupation, cafe manager and waitress, store manager, and teachers. Use schedules, ads, directories, parcel cards, and school minutes together. This converts an occupation list into a dated map of functions.
3. **Bracket 1952 and the interstate separately.** Seek business/tax/enrollment observations around 1948-51, 1953-60, and 1963-70. If a business disappeared before interstate construction, that changes its causal story. If it persisted until a documented access change, that is a different kind of evidence.
4. **Trace people directly across censuses and records.** Matched identities can establish a Cuervo-to-Santa Rosa move. A net loss in one unit and gain in another cannot. Include destinations outside Santa Rosa, particularly Tucumcari.
5. **Complete the earlier boundary reconstruction.** The 1950 ED geography is acquired; earlier precinct limits remain necessary. Avoid making the town more populous or its decline steeper simply by counting a large rural precinct.
6. **Explain Santa Rosa's 1930s growth on its own terms.** Earlier manuscript schedules, railway payrolls or station files, public works and government records, directories, and comparable retail censuses are needed. Cuervo's decline is not a sufficient explanation for another place gaining 1,183 residents.

## 11. What this can eventually offer planners

The emerging planning question is useful and more precise than “Does a highway kill a town?” It is: **Which local functions can adapt when access changes, which depend on a particular flow of customers, and what institutions give a place alternatives?**

This case suggests a research method: establish the economy before intervention; distinguish a settlement from its surrounding reporting district; examine business locations and access individually; separate local, regional, and through-traveler demand; and measure institutional change alongside population. Santa Rosa's growth and Cuervo's decline make a valuable comparison precisely because the records can reveal differences that a superficial description of “similar conditions” conceals.

At present, these are lessons about how to investigate planning outcomes. We have not estimated the benefit of any proposed intervention or established a population threshold for survival. A convincing contribution will explain both the businesses that disappeared and the households and services that persisted.
