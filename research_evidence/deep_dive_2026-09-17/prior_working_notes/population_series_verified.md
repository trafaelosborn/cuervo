## The corridor transect (natural experiment)

Five settlements on one ~55-mile line, sharing a rail line, Route 66, and I-40. Every one of them
has an interstate exit. Listed west to east.

| Settlement | County | 1930 | 1940 | 1950 | Today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Santa Rosa (town) | Guadalupe | 1,127 | 2,310 | 2,199 | survives |
| **Cuervo** (Prec. 6) | Guadalupe | 287 | 188 | 210 | ruin |
| Newkirk (Prec. 7) | Guadalupe | 239 | 271 | 190 | ruin |
| Montoya (Prec. 13) | Quay | 152 | 100 | 92 | ruin |
| Tucumcari (city) | Quay | — | — | 8,419 ‡ | survives |

Santa Rosa, Cuervo, and Newkirk read visually from the 1950 volume, Table 6, printed p. 31-8
(PDF p. 7). Montoya read visually from the same table, printed p. 31-9 (PDF p. 8), Quay County
block: `Prec. 13, Montoya — 92 | 100 | 152`.

‡ Tucumcari's figure is **OCR-only, not yet verified visually.** Quay County totals (13,971 /
12,111) are likewise unverified. Do not publish either until the page is rendered and read.

**Why this matters.** The three settlements that died were at 92–271 people by 1940. The two that
survived were above 2,000. Same railroad, same highway, same interstate, same interchange
geometry, same climate, same ranching hinterland — and the outcome sorts cleanly by mass, not by
road design. This is the strongest available control on any claim that I-40's engineering killed
Cuervo: if controlled access were the cause, Santa Rosa and Tucumcari should have died too.

**What it does not settle.** All three casualties were already far below any plausible
threshold to support a store, school, or church *before* I-40 was built (1963–65). The corridor
shows what the survival threshold was; it does not by itself show what set each place on its
trajectory. See caveat 8.

## Caveats that must travel with these numbers

1. **These are election-precinct counts, not town counts.** Cuervo was never separately
   enumerated as an incorporated place or CDP. The precinct is a much larger area than the
   settlement. Any chart labeled "population of Cuervo" would be false. Label it
   "Cuervo election precinct."

2. **The 1910→1920 precinct comparison is not census-sanctioned.** The 1920 volume's Table 2
   leaves the 1910 column blank for every Guadalupe precinct, and the headnote explains why:
   "counties showing no comparative figures for election precincts for 1910 or 1900 have been
   redistricted at some time between the censuses." The precinct numbering happens to match
   (Prec. 6 Cuervo in both years), but the Census Bureau itself declined to present them as a
   series. Treat 545 → 438 as indicative, not measured.

3. **The county series breaks in 1917.** De Baca County was organized from parts of Chaves,
   Guadalupe, and Roosevelt Counties in 1917 (1920 volume, Table 2, footnote 4 and footnote 8).
   Guadalupe County's 1910 figure of 10,927 covers territory the 1920 figure of 8,015 does not.
   The 1910→1920 county drop is mostly administrative, not demographic.

4. **Guadalupe County is itself a 1903 creation**, originally named Leonard Wood County and
   renamed in 1905, organized from parts of Bernalillo, Guadalupe, and Valencia Counties, with
   parts of old Guadalupe taken to form Quay and Roosevelt Counties (1910 volume, p. 164,
   footnotes 14 and 15). Pre-1910 county comparisons are close to meaningless.

5. **The 1930→1940 comparison, which is the load-bearing one, is clean.** All three geographies
   are presented in a single census table as a comparable series, with no boundary footnote
   affecting Cuervo or Santa Rosa. Santa Rosa's 1948 annexations (1950 volume, Guadalupe
   footnote) affect precincts 12 and 15, not 6 or 8.

6. **Santa Rosa is a confound as well as a comparison.** It is the county seat and was a
   railroad junction. It had absorbing functions the highway did not create. The 1930s doubling
   should not be attributed to Route 66 alone without further evidence.

7. **Dust Bowl context.** The 1940 census was taken in April 1940. Guadalupe County's 23% growth
   during the 1930s sits in a decade of drought displacement and New Deal programs in eastern
   New Mexico. The direction of that in-migration needs checking before the county figure is
   used as a baseline.

8. **The 1910–1930 decline may not be a Santa Rosa story at all.** Cuervo fell 545 → 438 → 287
   across two decades in which Santa Rosa was flat (precinct 1,390 → 1,381) and Guadalupe County
   also fell. A gravity/absorption mechanism predicts *divergence* — one place losing while a
   nearby larger place gains — and there is no divergence in this period. Eastern New Mexico had
   a dryland homestead boom c. 1907–1915 that collapsed in the 1920s drought, which would
   depopulate all of these precincts simultaneously and is the leading rival explanation for the
   early decline. Montoya (152 → 100 → 92) and Newkirk (239 → 271 → 190) show the same era of
   attrition. **Do not claim Santa Rosa was drawing people from Cuervo from the beginning until
   the 1910–1930 decline is separated from the homestead bust.** Test: occupation and birthplace
   coding from the manuscript census schedules, 1910 vs. 1920 vs. 1930 — a homestead bust shows
   as farmers leaving; an absorption story shows as merchants and service workers leaving.

9. **Cuervo's 1940→1950 uptick (188 → 210, +11.7%) is unexplained** and runs against a county
   falling 21.7% in the same decade. Do not smooth over it. Candidate explanations: postwar
   highway maintenance employment, a boundary adjustment not captured in the footnotes, or
   enumeration difference. Unresolved.

## What the shape shows

- **Cuervo declined in every decade from 1910 to 1940.** Its peak is the first census in which
  it appears. There is no Route 66 boom in the data at any point.
- **The 1930s is the divergence decade.** Santa Rosa town grew 105% and the county grew 23%
  while Cuervo fell 34.5%. Cuervo was not declining with its region; it was declining against it.
- **That decade is the Route 66 construction decade** — FAP 114-C realignment 1932, FAP 114
  hard-surfacing of the Montoya–Cuervo section 1935–36, New Mexico's Route 66 fully paved by
  the end of 1937 (both NRHP nominations, `sources/04_highways/`).
- **Santa Rosa was flat through the 1920s** (precinct −0.6%, town +12.4%) — the decade Route 66
  was *designated* (1926) but not yet realigned or paved. The growth arrives with the pavement,
  not the designation.
- **By 1940 Cuervo was down 65.5% from its 1910 figure** — twenty-three years before I-40
  construction began in 1963.

## OCR warning

None of these volumes can be mined by text extraction.

- The 1910, 1920, and 1940 PDFs have **no text layer at all**. They are raw scans.
- The 1950 PDF has a text layer, and it is **actively misleading**. `pdftotext` returns Cuervo's
  row as `105 164 186`. Those are Precinct 5, Colonias. The extractor shifted the row by one.
  Reading the OCR would have produced three wrong numbers with no indication anything was amiss.

Every figure in a published table must be confirmed against a rendered image of the page.

## Reproducing these reads

```python
import pymupdf
d = pymupdf.open('sources/03_census/us_census_1910_new_mexico_population_tables.pdf')
p = d[7]                                  # printed page 164
r = p.rect
clip = pymupdf.Rect(r.width*0.03, r.height*0.355, r.width*0.42, r.height*0.47)
p.get_pixmap(matrix=pymupdf.Matrix(9, 9), clip=clip).save('guadalupe_1910.png')
```

Page-number offsets: 1910 volume, printed = PDF + 156. 1920 volume, printed = PDF − 2.
1950 volume, Table 6 is PDF page 7.
