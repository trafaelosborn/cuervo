# Analytical Frame — Canonical Economics Applied to Cuervo

**Methodological stance (decided).** The monograph does not propose a law. It applies an
established body of theory as a diagnostic instrument and documents one case at a resolution the
existing literature cannot reach. Exceptions and anomalies are treated as probes that reveal the
model's boundary conditions, not as embarrassments to be explained away.

**Why this matters for the book.** The mechanism at work here is not a discovery. It has been
formalized since 1931 and measured empirically since at least 2000. The monograph's contribution
is documentary, not theoretical. Claiming otherwise would be both false and easy to puncture.

## The canonical stack

Citations verified August 2026. Where a claim is characterized rather than quoted, it is marked.

### 1. Reilly's Law of Retail Gravitation (1931)
William J. Reilly. Retail trade between two competing centers divides at a breaking point
determined by their relative populations and the distance between them; pull scales with mass and
decays with distance.

*Relevance:* the formal ancestor of the whole argument, and published in 1931 — between the two
censuses that bracket Cuervo's decisive decade. A book that names a model contemporary with the
pavement is doing something better than atmosphere.

### 2. Central place theory — Christaller (1933), Lösch (1940)
Settlements form a hierarchy. Each good or service has a **threshold** (minimum demand needed to
sustain it) and a **range** (maximum distance customers will travel for it). Higher-order goods
have higher thresholds and longer ranges, so they concentrate in fewer, larger places.

*Relevance:* predicts the *order* in which Cuervo's institutions die — highest threshold first.
Directly testable against the record: a hotel documented in 1915, no lodging by Rittenhouse's
1946 survey, while gas and groceries persist. Higher-order functions go first, lowest-order
convenience trade last. This is the single most testable prediction available from the archive.

### 3. Krugman, "Increasing Returns and Economic Geography" (1991)
*Journal of Political Economy* 99(3): 483–499. Core-periphery model; Nobel 2008.
When transport costs fall **below a critical threshold**, the symmetric equilibrium between two
locations becomes unstable and activity concentrates suddenly in one of them.

*Relevance — this is the important one.* The model predicts the **shape** of the curve, not just
its direction. Concentration is nonlinear: little happens, then a bifurcation, then rapid
separation. That is exactly what the verified series shows — Santa Rosa flat across the 1920s
(precinct 1,390 → 1,381), then +83% in the 1930s while Cuervo falls 34.5%. Flat, then snap.

*Test:* if the mechanism is Krugman-style bifurcation, the divergence should be abrupt and
should coincide with a discrete drop in travel cost — the 1935–36 hard-surfacing, not the 1926
designation. The data currently supports this. Higher-resolution evidence (business openings and
closings by year from the *Cuervo Clipper* and county records) would sharpen or break it.

### 4. Chandra & Thompson, "Does public infrastructure affect economic activity? Evidence from
the rural interstate highway system" (2000)
*Regional Science and Urban Economics* 30(4): 457–490.
Interstates **raise** economic activity in counties they pass directly through and **draw activity
away** from adjacent counties. The effect is spatial reallocation, not creation.

*Relevance and its limit:* the unit of analysis is the **county**. Cuervo and Santa Rosa are in
the same county. Within-county reallocation is invisible to this entire literature.
**That gap is where the monograph lives.** See "Where the book actually contributes," below.

### 5. The highway bypass literature
Consensus finding, characterized from multiple reviews (TRB *Transportation Research Record* 1395
and subsequent work): highway bypasses are "seldom either devastating or the savior" of a
community, and net economic impacts are usually small in either direction. Bypass effects must be
read **against a background of ongoing centralization of trade**, which the literature identifies
as the larger force. Size and distance to a larger city are the key moderating variables.

*Relevance:* this is the strongest external support for abandoning "I-40 severed Cuervo" as the
book's mechanism. The canonical empirical finding is that bypasses per se do not do what the
Route 66 folklore says they do. The folklore is the thing the book is correcting.

## The second mechanism: stop-spacing

Central place theory governs **residents**. It does not govern **through-traffic**, which is a
flow rather than a stock and does not depend on local population at all.

Roadside stop demand — fuel, food, bathroom, driver fatigue, restless children — is generated at
intervals of **time**, not distance. This has a consequence that runs opposite to intuition:

> A faster road does not reduce the number of stops. It stretches the spacing between viable
> stopping places.

At 45 mph a two-hour interval is 90 miles. At 70 mph the same interval is 140 miles. Raising the
speed thins the lattice of viable stop-points and concentrates traffic on the survivors.

**This is why Cuervo fails twice.** It is 20 miles from Santa Rosa and roughly 38 from Tucumcari.
At interstate speed that is 17 and 32 minutes. Cuervo sits *inside* the natural stop-spacing of
both. It is too close to Santa Rosa to be a central place, and too close to be a stopping place —
and both failures have the same cause: at 70 mph, 20 miles is nothing.

**Corollary (the fireworks-stand case).** A roadside business can survive on almost no local
population if it occupies a viable slot in the stop-spacing lattice *and* gives motorists a reason
to choose it over a generic stop — novelty, signage, a manufactured attraction. Such places are
not exceptions to the model. They are the model operating on a different population (motorists,
not residents) with a different threshold. Isolation is their asset. Cuervo has never been
isolated enough to qualify.

*This predicts something checkable:* surviving independent roadside businesses on NM's I-40
should cluster in the **long gaps** between service towns, not near them.

## Where the book actually contributes

The mechanism is well established. What is not established, anywhere in the literature, is the
settlement-level case.

1. **Resolution.** The empirical literature works at county or CBD scale because that is where
   the data is. Cuervo and Santa Rosa share a county. The archival campaign — plats, deeds,
   assessor cards, board minutes, business registers — supplies a sub-county resolution the
   econometrics cannot reach.
2. **Duration.** A century-long single-place series, verified figure by figure.
3. **A controlled corridor.** Five settlements, one 55-mile transect, identical rail line,
   identical highway, identical interstate, five different masses, two survivors. See
   `population_series_verified.md`.
4. **Named decisions.** The literature has coefficients. The monograph can have the actual
   project number, the actual letting date, the actual board vote that moved the children to
   Santa Rosa.

## Available empirical move (optional, appendix-scale)

Plot 1950 population against survival-to-present for every settlement on New Mexico's I-40
corridor. With enough points this yields an estimated **survival threshold** — the approximate
1950 population below which a New Mexico interstate settlement did not persist. That is a real,
citable finding, it is cheap (published census plus present-day status), and it directly serves
the Society's "rigorous fieldwork" positioning.

*Discipline:* run it, put it in an appendix, keep the narrative on Cuervo. It should not be
allowed to convert the book into a regional study. The plan's open Cuervo-only-vs-exemplar
question is answered by letting generality remain an implication rather than a claim.

## The standing risk

Economic determinism reads as fate. "Cuervo was doomed by threshold effects" and "Cuervo was
doomed by destiny" land on a reader in exactly the same place — and the second one is the elegy
the project plan explicitly forbids. A model that explains everything absolves everyone, and an
absolving book is a sentimental book wearing a lab coat.

The antidote is contingency: the decisions were made by people, on dates, in documents.
Someone chose the 1932 alignment. Someone let the 1935 paving contract. Someone voted to
consolidate the school. Someone signed project I-040-5(18)1283. The economics explains the
pressure; the archive supplies the hands. Keep both, and the book stays an argument rather than
a lament.
