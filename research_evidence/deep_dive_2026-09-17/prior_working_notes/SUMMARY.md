## The argument, as it now stands

**Cuervo was a friction town. It existed because Santa Rosa was just far enough away, and it died
as that distance got cheaper.**

Twenty miles is a hard day's round trip by wagon and seventeen minutes at interstate speed.
Cuervo's reason for existing was the cost of covering that gap; every improvement to the road
eroded it and transferred the town's functions to the county seat twenty miles west.

The evidence is a **divergence**, not a decline. In the 1930s — the decade Route 66 was realigned
and paved — Cuervo fell 34.5% while Santa Rosa town grew 105% and Guadalupe County grew 23%.
Cuervo was not emptying with its region. It was emptying into Santa Rosa.

The mechanism is ordinary economic geography, a century old, and the book should say so plainly.
Its contribution is documentary: one settlement at a resolution the economics literature has
never reached, because that literature works at county scale and Cuervo and Santa Rosa share a
county.

**The corridor** — five settlements, one 55-mile transect, shared rail line, Route 66, and I-40,
every one with an interchange. Survival sorts by mass, not road design: Santa Rosa (2,199) and
Tucumcari survive; Cuervo (210), Newkirk (190), Montoya (92) do not.

**Interstate** — project I-040-5(18)1283, MP 282.7 to ~290.7, initiated 1963, completed 1965.
Traffic at the Cuervo interchange 11,415–20,051 AADT, 32–50% commercial, 1993–2001.

**Institutions** — post office discontinued 10 Sept 2011, ZIP 88417 retained, administration to
Newkirk. The town itself is not on the National Register; two road segments are (1993 and 1997).

**Disproved folklore** — the CRI&G founding attribution is chronologically impossible; rail
service did not end in 1910; "abandoned in 2011" is false; the "58 people in 2000" figure is
ZCTA data, not a town count. Full myth audit in the scaffold, and it is strong enough to be
publishable material rather than back matter.

## What is assumed and must not be published yet

- **The 1910–1930 decline is probably not a Santa Rosa story.** No divergence appears in that
  period — Santa Rosa was flat and the county fell too. The leading rival explanation is the
  eastern New Mexico dryland homestead bust. Two mechanisms in sequence, not yet separated.
- **Whether rail-era Cuervo was already inside Santa Rosa's reach.** Testable against the 1926
  timetable already held: did the schedule permit a day return?
- **Which ruin is which.** No standing structure has been matched to a name, function, or date.
  The school, the churches, the stores are presently unidentified.
- **Anything about water scarcity.** Plausible, unproved, and currently doing no work.

**Permanent guard rail:** the population series is an *election-precinct* series. Cuervo was never
separately enumerated as a place. A chart labeled "population of Cuervo" would be false.

## What exists

**Documentary — strong.** 324 files retrieved against 10 failures, SHA-256 manifest, both NRHP
nominations, the 1926 Rock Island timetable, four census volumes, USGS topo sheets for 1963 and
2023, the NTSB brief and factual report, the USPS bulletin, and an index of all 270 *Cuervo
Clipper* issues with previews.

**Photographic — scouting stage.** 73 iPhone frames from a single winter midday walk-through.
Near-duplicate bursts, sun in frame, two blank sky exposures, GPS present but timestamps
stripped, no drone frames despite the plan specifying a DJI Air 3, and several interiors shot
without established permission. The strongest frames are the I-40 culvert, which is the argument
photographed, though the "thats fine" graffiti is a trap: it is the most shareable image here and
the most ruin-porn-adjacent.

**The imbalance is the project's central problem.** The plan's governing principle is that if
either half could be removed without loss, the balance has failed. Right now the essay could
stand without the plates.

## The bottleneck, stated plainly

**A day at the Guadalupe County courthouse and a second field trip in decent light.** That is the
whole critical path. Everything else is either done or waiting on those two.

Neither is blocked by software.

The live risk is the Folio detour. Building a page-composition system to publish a 128-page book
means building the last phase first, and the months it costs are months not spent on the records
campaign and the reshoot. Affinity became free in October 2025 — the three apps are now one
application with the old Publisher toolset as its layout persona — which removes the cost argument
entirely. Folio is a defensible product idea on its own; it should not be this book's dependency.
If the book ships when Folio ships, then the artifact meant to prove the Society can finish
rigorous fieldwork instead proves it cannot.

## Standing risks

- **Determinism reading as fate.** "Doomed by threshold effects" and "doomed by destiny" land on a
  reader identically, and the second is the elegy the plan forbids. The pressure was structural;
  the decisions had names, dates, and signatures. Keep both.
- **The seductive false chart.** A clean declining line labeled "population of Cuervo" is the most
  likely place this book publishes something untrue.
- **OCR.** The 1950 census PDF has a text layer that returns Cuervo's row as another precinct's
  numbers, with no indication anything is wrong. That is how the 645 got in. Render the page.
- **The 50/50 rule.** The archive is now far ahead of the plates. That is the failure the book
  would not survive.
