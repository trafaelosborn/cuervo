# The whole census history: Cuervo and its regional comparisons

*Research update for Taylor Osborn, September 18, 2026. Extends the earlier census report backward to the first named Cuervo precinct schedules and retains the published comparisons through 2020.*

> **September 19 correction and extension:** enlarged review of 1950 sheet 1 corrects Ruth Parker's age (line 2) from 58 to 38 and Henry Yates's (line 23) from 24 to 34. The revised 1950 median is **25.5**, rather than 25; adult age bins and cohort comparisons using those readings also change. Population and child counts are unchanged. This report, its figures, original JSON and PDF remain the September 18 audit version; use the [rebound brief](rebound_1940_1950_2026-09-19.md) and [revised person data](rebound_1940_1950_2026-09-19/people_name_age_readings.json) for the corrected 1940/1950 analysis. That extension also supplies complete first-pass names/grouping and selected household links.

## 1. What the longer study has already changed

**Cuervo became smaller without becoming steadily older before World War II.** The new manuscript readings put its median recorded age at 20-21 in 1910, 21 in 1920, 18-19 in 1930, and 18 in 1940. It rises to 25 in 1950. In 1940, at least 90 of 188 residents were under 18. These results describe the Cuervo precinct, including rural territory, rather than just the surviving town buildings. [C1-C5]

That matters for the proposed explanation that young people had already left and an elderly remnant consequently supplied fewer wartime servicemen. The precinct still contained many children and working-age adults in 1940. Its age profile does not support a simple story of progressive aging before the war. **Whether Cuervo was older than Santa Rosa in relative terms remains untested:** the matching Santa Rosa age transcription is not complete.

The expanded study now has:

- **Every Cuervo manuscript census from 1910 through 1950 indexed and read for age and recorded sex.** The five counts reconcile to 545, 438, 287, 188, and 210.
- **1,668 census appearances**, with 1,661 readable ages and seven unresolved ages. These are appearances across five censuses, not 1,668 different people. Names have not yet been linked across years.
- **Ten newly downloaded county microfilms, 11,471 PDF pages, 4.65 GB**, covering 1900-1930 regional and comparison populations. Each file has a download URL, byte count, page count, and SHA-256 hash.
- **Published population and housing comparisons through 2020**, incorporated from the preceding report. The acquisition and the completed analysis are distinguished throughout this report.

![Published population and manually read median ages, Cuervo precinct](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/census_full_history_2026-09-18/population_and_age.png)

The median ranges show the entire effect that the unresolved ages could have on that statistic, conditional on the other readings being correct. They are not statistical confidence intervals. Early precinct and county boundary changes still require reconstruction.

### What we can infer

The absolute number of children fell alongside the total population, while their share stayed substantial. The earlier decline therefore cannot be described adequately as a town simply retaining its elderly residents while everyone young disappeared. Births, children aging into adulthood, deaths, and migration all shape these distributions. A falling number of children is not itself a count of children who moved away.

The 1940-1950 increase also deserves its own explanation. Total population rose by 22, while the under-18 count fell by five or six. The count aged 18 and over consequently rose by 27 or 28. The population was larger and older at the later endpoint. This is consistent with several combinations of cohort aging, adult arrivals, departures, births, and deaths; it does not establish a wartime employment effect.

The first 1910 sheet contains farming families and several household members born in states outside New Mexico. That is a useful lead for studying settlement and household origins, but birthplace has not been coded for the entire precinct. We cannot yet report an immigrant or newcomer percentage from those examples. [C1, sheet 1A]

## 3. The war hypothesis now has a prewar baseline

The 1940 reading contains **36 people recorded as male aged 18-44**, plus **14 boys aged 12-17**. One additional male has an unread age. These counts are demographic reference groups, not a count of people eligible for the draft, inducted, absent in military service, or killed. The age range is an analytical choice, not a reconstruction of changing draft rules. [C4]

Three implications follow:

1. **The small population could yield few servicemen in absolute numbers without being unusually old.** Population size and age composition must be distinguished.
2. **The teenage group matters.** Some boys recorded in 1940 would reach military-service ages during the war. Looking only at adults on the census date would miss them.
3. **Census endpoints do not measure the wartime peak.** Neither the 1940 nor the 1950 count directly observes changes in freight, cattle shipments, temporary employment, service departures, or return migration during the intervening years.

The next direct test is a comparable Santa Rosa age distribution, followed by person-level checks against military, railway, ranching, and residence records. The current data do not establish how many residents served or whether Cuervo sent a smaller proportion than Santa Rosa.

## 4. What “the whole tamale” can mean in the surviving records

| Period | What is available | Current position |
|---|---|---|
| Before 1850 | Selected Spanish and Mexican period censuses in the State Archives | Cataloged research route, not acquired or treated as a Cuervo population series |
| 1850, 1860, 1870, 1880 | Federal population schedules for the territorial era | State Archives holdings verified; use for regional and family origins after identifying the relevant historical jurisdiction |
| 1885 | New Mexico territorial census: population, agriculture, manufactures, mortality | NARA M846 guide verified; potentially useful regional baseline, not yet transcribed |
| 1890 | Published statistics and some special schedules survive | No New Mexico general-population schedules appear in NARA's surviving-fragment list; veterans schedules are not a population substitute |
| 1900 | Guadalupe and Valencia county population microfilms | Downloaded; regional headers inspected, full household transcription not completed |
| 1910-1950 | Named Cuervo precinct schedules in all five decades | Complete age/sex reading coverage; seven ages unresolved; person and household linkage still pending |
| 1960-2020 | Published population, housing, and other aggregate census products | Population comparisons assembled; household/group-quarters comparisons available for selected later decades |

The [State Archives census holdings](https://www.srca.nm.gov/primary-sources/) and [NARA's 1885 guide](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/census/nonpopulation/m846.pdf) establish the earlier research routes. M846 assigns San Miguel to roll 4 and Valencia to roll 6; those are finding aids, not evidence that a particular earlier district matches Cuervo's later boundary. The [1890 records guide](https://www.archives.gov/research/census/1890) distinguishes the surviving general-population fragments from the special veterans schedules.

**1950 is the latest public named household census.** Later published totals can be studied now; the National Archives schedules release of the 1960 individual records for April 2032. There is no continuous publicly available named-household series through 2020. [NARA release notice](https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/1960-census-work-begins)

### The 1900 baseline is regional, not “Cuervo equals zero”

The 1910 published table retrospectively reports **5,429** for Guadalupe County in 1900. Its footnote describes substantial changes in county composition in 1903 and a name change from Leonard Wood in 1905. That number is not a constant-boundary predecessor to the 1910 county total of 10,927. [C6, 1910 Table 1, printed 164]

The 1900 reel also shows different locality labels: the sheets around PDF pages 855-857 label precinct 6 and show La Cuesta, crossed through, in the place-name line; pages 858 onward identify precinct 7 as Las Colonias. The later Cuervo precinct number cannot simply be projected backward. Santa Rosa appears as precinct 8 in the 1900 schedules, including PDF 870, but matching a name or number still does not establish an unchanged boundary. [C7]

Earlier regional households can help identify who later settled around Cuervo. A missing named Cuervo category does not mean the surrounding land had no residents.

## 5. Putting the early findings beside the later population history

The published Cuervo precinct count falls from 545 in 1910 to 188 in 1940, then rises to 210 in 1950. The 1910-1920 comparison remains affected by redistricting. The better-documented 1930-1940 comparison shows Cuervo losing 99 residents while Santa Rosa municipality gained 1,183 and Guadalupe County gained 1,619. These are changes in net counts, not observed transfers of people.

### Santa Rosa and Los Lunas represent different later outcomes

| Census | Santa Rosa household population | Los Lunas household population |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1980 | 2,467 | 3,158 |
| 1990 | 2,240 | 5,936 |
| 2000 | 2,241 | 9,920 |
| 2010 | 2,280 | 14,688 |
| 2020 | 2,261 | 16,994 |

Santa Rosa's total population increased by 587 from 1990 to 2020, but household population increased by only 21. Group quarters account for 566 of the net increase. The 2020 census classifies 585 city residents in adult correctional facilities. That does not mean all group-quarters residents were prisoners or that the institution had no wider effect on household stability. [C8]

Los Lunas's household population increased 186.3% over the same interval. Its municipal footprint also expanded. Commuting, local employment, and annexation evidence are needed to distinguish metropolitan residential growth from growth produced by local jobs or changed municipal limits. [C8]

The full history therefore gives us several outcomes to explain: Cuervo's early contraction, a surviving family community in 1950, Santa Rosa's later residential stability amid county decline, and Los Lunas's substantial household expansion. A single pair of rising and falling total-population curves cannot settle the “big fish” hypothesis.

### The geographic break after 1950

The later published county-subdivision tables use census county divisions rather than the old election precincts. Neither the inspected 2010 nor 2020 place files contains a named Cuervo place total. A modern Cuervo count needs an explicit boundary and block extraction. The Santa Rosa division, Newkirk's modern census-designated place, and the historic Cuervo precinct are different populations.

This report consequently leaves Cuervo's post-1950 local total unresolved. It does not fill the gap with a neighboring place, a large division, or a zero.

## 7. Reading rules and checks

**Coverage.** All named rows within the identified Cuervo precinct pages are represented. Empty lines and an entirely blank 1940 reverse are excluded. The 1930 sheet 1B has 49 names, leaving printed line 100 blank; sheet 3B has a late additional entry at printed line 90. In 1940, printed line 70 on sheet 1B is blank. The 1950 callback sheet is included.

**Ages.** Ages are transcribed as written. Infants under one are coded zero; fractional ages above one use completed years. A blank or unclear value remains unknown. An age corrected in the manuscript is read from the legible correction where possible. We do not force ten-year age increments between unlinked individuals.

**Recorded sex.** The dataset preserves written M/F entries, including apparent contradictions with household relationships. For example, 1910 sheet 5A line 6 says daughter but records M; 1940 sheet 1A line 40 has an overwritten sex entry read as M despite wife in the relationship column. The earlier 1950 transcription has a similar recorded discrepancy. The report's principal findings concern age, not sex ratios.

**Unresolved ages.** The seven records are 1910 sheet 2A line 22; 1930 sheet 1A lines 8, 24, and 25, sheet 2B line 91, and sheet 3A line 28; and 1940 sheet 2B line 63. They remain visible in the machine-readable observations. The 1910 cell is blank; several 1930 cells are faint; the 1940 age is faint and circled.

**Sensitivity.** For median bounds, the script assigns every unknown age zero, then 110, and recomputes the median. This deliberately broad test leaves the 1940 median at 18 either way. For under-18 bounds, it tests none versus all of the unknown ages being children. These bounds address unread ages only, not errors in otherwise accepted readings, enumeration mistakes, omissions, or changed geography.

**Independent review.** This is one manual transcription with targeted rechecks, not a completed independent double-entry transcription. It should receive a second reading before publication-level demographic estimates or person-matching decisions rely on individual cells. Count reconciliation and arithmetic are complete.

### A concrete warning in the migration columns

The 1940 schedules offer April 1, 1935 residence, which is valuable for migration research. But the Cuervo entries need an audit before counting origins: sheet 1A contains “R” entries with county and state left blank, and sheet 1B line 55 records “Same house” for a person whose recorded age is one. A child that young cannot supply a valid personal 1935 residence observation. [C4]

The questionnaire uses R for places below its specified population threshold; R does not identify a particular origin. The [NARA description of the 1940 form](https://www.archives.gov/research/census/1940/general-info) explains the residence and employment questions. We have not converted those problematic entries into migration counts. Birthplace, 1935 residence, and disappearance from a later local census must remain separate variables.

The 1950 cover's field total of 210 versus Washington-office total of 211, with one ICR noted, remains unresolved. The 210 visible named entries match the published precinct total; this does not explain the administrative discrepancy. [C5]

## 8. The remaining work is specific

The expanded census collection is ready for a household study across all five decades. The age series is complete in coverage. The following components are still needed to establish movements and mechanisms:

1. **Names, households, and record linkage, 1910-1950.** Transcribe names, relationships, birthplace, and occupations; match families using several fields. Keep confirmed, probable, ambiguous, and unmatched cases separate. A matching surname is insufficient.
2. **Santa Rosa and Los Lunas comparisons.** Begin with the 1940 age comparison, then apply consistent occupation and household classifications. Preserve institutions and surrounding precincts as separate groups.
3. **Where departing households went.** Search beyond Santa Rosa, including Tucumcari, other New Mexico places, and out-of-state destinations. Only a located later record establishes a destination. An unmatched resident could have moved, died, changed a name, been missed, or become difficult to identify.
4. **A geographic bridge.** Reconstruct early precinct changes and the 1950 district, then define a separate modern settlement footprint. Extract later census blocks against explicit boundaries and show the effect of alternative reasonable boundaries.
5. **Economic and institutional timing.** Relate occupations and household moves to rail functions, agricultural change, schools, churches, businesses, and dated highway changes. Decennial demographics alone do not tell us which event caused a move or closure.

The 1920 Santa Rosa municipal discrepancy also remains open: the contemporary volume appears to print 1,003, while two 1930 tables give 1,093 retrospectively. It is excluded from change calculations here. The county and precinct values are separate observations.

**Working conclusion:** the longer record strengthens the evidence for a declining but still family-populated community before the interstate. It makes the explanation more demanding: we need to account for shrinking livelihoods and regional roles without assuming that Cuervo had already become an elderly remnant, that its missing residents all went to Santa Rosa, or that Los Lunas's later expansion arose from the same process.
