# Chapter 1
# Reading Cuervo

In September 1911, a new school was going up in Cuervo. At the same time, Big Jo Lumber was shipping stock out of town and cutting prices on what remained. The railway had also widened and improved the depot platform. All three developments appeared on the front page of the *Cuervo Clipper*.[^1]

The school was expanding while the lumber firm was withdrawing stock. The railway was improving its facilities, but that offered no guarantee to nearby businesses. Even this small settlement could grow in one respect and shrink in another.

Today, Cuervo is commonly described as a Route 66 ghost town. Its surviving buildings help explain the label. The surrounding land, however, still supports ranching. In a January 2026 interview, Jim Armendariz described his father's purchase of a ranch outside Cuervo in 2020. That July, Rancho Armendariz of Cuervo became a finalist for the New Mexico Leopold Conservation Award. Ranching belongs to Cuervo's present as well as its past.[^2]

That contrast is the central problem of this book. The land could remain productive while the town lost shops, services and residents. To explain how that happened, the history must begin with what made Cuervo useful. People came to trade, attend school and obtain services. Each activity had its own reasons for operating there. Each depended on people and resources that could move elsewhere.

## Roads and roadside trade

The familiar highway story is simple: Route 66 brought trade, and Interstate 40 took it away. The newer road is much larger than the settlement beside it. Its physical presence makes it easy to imagine one decisive turning point.

Published accounts give that turning point different roles. The National Trust for Historic Preservation identifies interstate construction as the beginning of Cuervo's decline. Jim Winnerman's 2022 account in *Wild West* describes earlier railway disappointment. He also says that most residents had left before the interstate arrived. These accounts disagree about whether the new road began the decline or dealt a later blow.[^3]

A road change can hurt businesses in different ways. A filling station may rely on passing drivers. A merchant supplying ranchers may serve customers who deliberately travel there. A hotel needs people with a reason to stay overnight. An interchange keeps a place connected to the road, but it cannot guarantee customers for each business.

At Cuervo, some losses came much earlier. The lumber-stock withdrawal was reported in 1911. In 1922, a newspaper proprietor argued for concentrating publication in Santa Rosa. The population reported for the Cuervo precinct fell sharply during the 1930s. All three events preceded the documented interstate project of the 1960s. The first two even preceded Route 66's designation in 1926.[^4]

The interstate therefore cannot explain the whole history. It could still have hurt businesses that survived those earlier changes. Those later effects need to be considered on their own evidence. An early decline does not make a later loss harmless.

Changes to the cars also deserve attention. Air conditioning made travel in hot weather more comfortable. ASHRAE, the professional society for heating and cooling engineers, records it in 54% of new cars by 1969.[^5] A cooler car could make it easier to continue to the next town. It could reduce the appeal of a stop made mainly for relief from the heat. A journey could become less tiring even when the road stayed in place.

That is a plausible explanation for some later roadside losses, not an established cause of a particular Cuervo closure. The available records do not measure its local effect. More comfortable travel could also encourage more trips, giving roadside businesses additional potential customers. The result would depend on both the amount of traffic and where drivers chose to spend. For a roadside business, traffic passing the door and customers coming through it are different measures.

Cuervo's earlier businesses served other needs. A merchant traded there before Route 66 existed. Families came for school. These activities help explain why a town formed at all. The highway entered a place that already had an economy.

## A store with a wide reach

The early merchant offers a clear example. In 1958, Frank H. Grubbs wrote a study of the Bond enterprises. He traced the Cuervo business to a predecessor at Cabra in 1899. The move to Cuervo was complete by the end of 1903. Grubbs connected it with access to the railway.[^6]

The Cuervo firm did several jobs. It sold goods, extended credit and dealt in sheep and wool. In March 1910, the *Clipper* reported fourteen cars of sheep loaded at Cuervo for Kansas. The following week's issue reported Bond & Wiest unloading separate cars of flour, sugar and grain.[^7] The railway carried agricultural goods out and supplies in.

Much of the work that supported this trade took place outside the village. Ranchers needed access to distant buyers. They also needed goods that arrived from elsewhere. Cuervo brought these transactions together. Its small resident population was only part of the merchant's market.

This helps explain why the size of a town can be a poor guide to the reach of its businesses. A store might serve people across a large area. Its customers might come only occasionally and make substantial purchases. Counting nearby residents would miss that trade. The important question is who used the business and what they used it for.

The merchant also did more than sell finished goods. Credit helped customers finance their activities. Dealings in sheep and wool connected production to buyers. Combining these tasks in one firm may have offered advantages to both merchant and customer. The records establish that the firm performed them. They do not, by themselves, show how much money the combination saved.

Grubbs reconstructed the company's early finances from business records. His figures show positive net profits in every year from 1904 through 1915. Profits fell in 1910 and 1911, then recovered. He also described the Cuervo business as still operating in 1958.[^6] That later observation establishes survival, although it does not tell the financial story of every intervening year.

The long-lived merchant matters because a town's businesses need not share the same fate. One could remain useful while another lost customers or financial support. A profitable firm could also serve a wide area without creating many jobs or purchases in the village itself. Business success and town growth were related, but one did not guarantee the other.

This distinction helps frame the history of ranching. Productive land created opportunities for trade. It did not determine where that trade would take place or how many local services it would support. Those outcomes depended on the choices available to producers, customers and business owners.

## What kept a service open?

The school gave families another reason to come to Cuervo. In September 1910, the *Clipper* reported sixty-seven pupils. It also said that families were moving in from the surrounding country so their children could attend. By April 1912, the paper described a new two-story school, two teachers and further moves for schooling.[^8]

The direction of cause matters here. It is easy to assume that a school followed the arrival of enough families. These reports show that the school itself attracted families. A service could help build the population on which other businesses might depend.

The newspaper's own finances reveal a different source of support. On December 1, 1922, Katharine A. Hicks announced that she was ending her financial connection with the *Clipper*. She reported substantial losses. In her judgment, the county could support one first-class weekly, preferably at the county seat. Her associate Charles Gerhardt planned improvements to his Santa Rosa paper, the *Public Voice*.[^9]

Hicks was making a specific argument about publishing. She believed that support for a good weekly should be concentrated in one place. Santa Rosa offered the advantage of being the county seat. This was her assessment of the newspaper market, made before Route 66 received its designation.

Her decision also involved politics. The purchase of the *Clipper* had returned it to Democratic alignment and helped the party's county and state candidates. Hicks considered those purposes fulfilled. She treated the financial loss as a campaign contribution.

The paper had therefore served more than a commercial purpose. Its losses were acceptable while its political work justified them to the owner. Understanding why that support ended requires both parts of her explanation. The number of potential readers alone cannot account for the decision.

Hicks's announcement explains why one person stopped financing the paper. Its exact final publication date remains a separate question.

The school, newspaper and merchant depended on different arrangements. The school drew families into town. The newspaper received support for political work. The merchant served a rural market through several lines of business. These activities helped sustain the same settlement, but they could prosper or struggle for different reasons.

## Which Cuervo is being counted?

The name Cuervo does not always mean the same area. A photograph may show the village's buildings. A census table may count residents across a larger district. A merchant's customers may live farther away still. These differences matter when the sources are compared.

The published population of Cuervo election precinct 6 was 287 in 1930, 188 in 1940 and 210 in 1950. The precinct included land beyond the built settlement. These are therefore counts of the precinct's residents, including people outside the village. Comparisons with earlier censuses face further problems because reporting areas changed.[^10]

The figures show a large fall followed by a smaller rise. They put population loss before the interstate. They also show that the reported population did not fall in every decade. The increase from 1940 to 1950 was twenty-two people. In a population this small, a few households could noticeably change the total.

A census is taken at intervals. It cannot show every arrival, departure, birth and death between those dates. Several combinations of events can produce the same net change. A business could close during a decade when population rose. Another could invest while population fell. The totals alone cannot identify a cause for either movement.

Where people live is also different from where they obtain services. A family could remain near Cuervo while traveling elsewhere for school or shopping. The loss of a local service might first change its journeys and spending. A move of residence could come later, or never happen. These are separate changes, even when one influences another.

Railway administration provides another example. Rock Island's December 1974 station book listed Cuervo without an agent and assigned its billing to Tucumcari. Santa Rosa's billing was also assigned there.[^11] For this railway function, both towns depended on a center farther east.

The entry tells where billing was assigned. It does not establish whether freight was actually shipped or how much moved. Its value here is the relationship it identifies. Santa Rosa did not handle every function associated with Cuervo merely because it was the larger neighbor.

Different services can have different centers. Residents might use one town for shopping, another for a specialized service and a wider network for selling livestock. The region's economy cannot be understood by assigning each small settlement to one larger town. Its connections have to be followed separately.

This also changes the meaning of local decline. A service can leave a town while remaining available to its residents elsewhere. That can still impose costs: longer journeys, fewer nearby jobs and fewer reasons to visit. Continued access to a service does not mean that its departure left the town unaffected.

## The economics of a small place

Keeping a business open costs money. Premises, equipment and workers must be paid for. Customers must also be able and willing to reach it. A school or newspaper has its own costs and sources of support. Cuervo's history shows why those requirements need to be examined one activity at a time.

Adam Smith linked specialization to the size of the market. Put simply, a specialist needs enough work to make a living. Where demand is small, one person or firm may perform several tasks. Where demand is larger, separate businesses may be able to support themselves.[^12]

Cuervo gives that idea a concrete setting. Its merchant combined several lines of work and reached customers beyond the village. The relevant market was larger than the local population. This helps explain how a substantial enterprise could operate in a small settlement.

Central-place theory asks how towns supply their surrounding areas with goods and services. Different activities need different numbers of customers and draw them from different distances. A frequently used, inexpensive service may survive on a relatively small market. A costly specialist may need customers from a much wider area.[^12]

This is a useful starting point, but the history adds other forms of support. A newspaper owner may subsidize losses for political reasons. Decisions about school provision can affect where families settle. Demand from paying customers is only part of the explanation for which services remain available.

Agricultural growth raises a related question: where does the income go? A rancher earning more might buy supplies locally, purchase equipment elsewhere or invest in the ranch. Higher livestock receipts need not produce another shop in Cuervo. Douglass North's work on agriculture and regional growth explains why exports alone cannot guarantee a more varied local economy. How production is organized and where income is spent also matter.[^12]

Transport can change these choices in several directions. Better connections may help a local merchant reach more customers and obtain cheaper supplies. They may also help those customers reach a larger competing store. Easier, more comfortable journeys can change which trip seems worthwhile. A town can become better connected while losing some of its local trade.

Services can also support one another. A place with a school, jobs and shops offers several reasons to live there. Losing one may make residence less attractive or reduce the customers available to another. This is a possible process of cumulative decline: separate losses can make further losses more likely.

The Cuervo evidence does not establish every link in such a chain. It does show that services differed in what they needed to survive. A particular worker or financial backer could matter greatly. Population alone was not enough to explain whether an activity continued.

There is consequently no single population figure that this book can identify as Cuervo's point of no return. A subsidized service can survive with few customers. Another can fail because a worker leaves, even when demand remains. New staff, funding or ways of operating can change the prospects again. Vulnerability does not make every loss permanent.

The strongest conclusion concerns the changing work of the town. Cuervo brought trade and services together for people across a wider territory. Some activities lasted; others withdrew or were organized elsewhere. As local services diminished, the town offered fewer reasons to gather there. The record supports that account more firmly than an exact division of responsibility among roads, agriculture and institutional decisions.

## What the buildings can show

The surviving buildings are an important part of the evidence. Photographs show their materials, condition and position in the landscape. They can record damage precisely. They usually cannot date a business's departure or explain why it closed.

The photographs therefore need to be read alongside other records. School reports document investment and arrivals. Merchant accounts connect a building to customers and distant markets. Current ranching records establish work beyond the village. Together, these sources describe more of the place than its buildings alone can reveal.

Other scholars have examined places where visible decay coexists with continued use and attachment. Studies of former mining communities distinguish visitors' impressions from residents' experiences. Rural geographers have described several kinds of abandonment and reuse, including declining settlements surrounded by active agriculture.[^13] Cuervo belongs within this wider history.

This book's contribution is to reconstruct how its particular economy worked. The following chapters examine the town's formation, trade, services, population and transport. They then bring those findings together to explain its decline. Investment, recovery and survival remain part of that explanation.

Cuervo lost many of the activities that had made it a local center. The surrounding country remained productive and connected to distant markets. Explaining how those histories diverged begins with an earlier question: why did people gather at this particular place?
