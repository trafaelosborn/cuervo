# Home cooling, Cuervo and Santa Rosa: research sweep

**September 24, 2026 | Meridian research memorandum**

## Finding

Home cooling belongs in the account of changing living conditions. The evidence does not make it a leading explanation for Cuervo's loss of town services, or establish that its effect was negligible.

The strongest new finding is local. By 1980, the census reported air conditioning in **66.1% of Santa Rosa's year-round housing**. The town had slightly fewer residents than in 1970 and would lose more over the next decade. Cooling could coexist with population stagnation. It did not guarantee growth.

The user's comparison remains useful: a comfort improvement available in both places cannot, by itself, explain why their services developed differently. But equal historical access and cost are assumptions. We have found county and Santa Rosa adoption evidence, not an equivalent Cuervo series.

There is also an important correction to the measurement: **the 1960, 1970 and 1980 census definitions excluded evaporative coolers.** These tables are not a complete history of household cooling in New Mexico.

### Denominators and definitions matter

The 1960 county observation comprises 25 occupied units with one room unit, 17 with two or more, none shown with central systems, and 1,368 with no AC. This reconciles to 1,410. The 1970 categories are 340, 18, 120 and 1,171 respectively. They total 1,649, while the table's headline year-round housing total is 1,646. That is not a transcription correction: Appendix C, Table A identifies a **15% sample for AC and a 20% sample for housing totals**. The rate above uses the AC categories' own denominator.

The move from occupied units in 1960 to year-round housing in 1970 and 1980 also changes the population being measured. Later denominators include vacant year-round units. We should not present these three percentages as a fully harmonized adoption curve or treat their differences as exact percentage-point changes for occupied households.

All three definitions restrict AC to refrigeration equipment. See the 1960 general definitions, printed XXXVI/PDF 22; 1970 Appendix B, App-11/PDF 11; and 1980 Appendix B, B-7/PDF 31. The latter explicitly includes heat pumps. The printed rates remain **reported equipment under those definitions**, not independently verified inventories. Misclassification by respondents is possible. Biddle specifically raises this concern about evaporative coolers in the 1960 responses.

Santa Rosa and Los Lunas have almost identical published 1980 AC percentages. This is a useful descriptive comparison, not a matched experiment. Their climates, employment access, boundaries, housing histories and household populations differ. Nor is Guadalupe County a substitute for Cuervo: the county figure includes Santa Rosa itself.

There is no separate Cuervo cooling row in these checked publications. The 1970 detailed place tables begin at 2,500 inhabitants; Santa Rosa's published population was 2,485. The 1980 governmental-unit summary supplies the later Santa Rosa observation despite its size. A lack of a town row must not be recorded as zero adoption.

The exact readings and universes are retained in [cooling_observations.json](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/home_cooling_2026-09-24/cooling_observations.json).

| 1960 | 2,220 | 5,610 |
| 1970 | 2,485 | 4,969 |
| 1980 | 2,469 | 4,496 |
| 1990 | 2,263 | 4,156 |

These are the project's previously checked published counts, with original report and page locators in [manual_published_readings.json](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/census_deep_dive_2026-09-18/manual_published_readings.json). They are reported administrative geographies, not a new constant-boundary reconstruction.

Santa Rosa gained 21 residents in the 1950s, gained 265 in the 1960s, lost 16 in the 1970s and lost 206 in the 1980s. Across 1970-1990, its reported population fell about 8.9%. The county's population fell about 33.6% between 1950 and 1980, during the broad diffusion of household AC.

This weakens a simple local story in which cooling triggered sustained population expansion. It does **not** identify what the population would have been without cooling. A technology that made life easier could have reduced losses, supported older residents, or helped households remain on ranches. Those effects are possible and unmeasured here.

Cuervo's earlier changes require a different clock. The held Clipper material records commercial changes before World War I and Hicks's financial and political withdrawal in 1922. Cuervo precinct population fell from 287 in 1930 to 188 in 1940, then rose to 210 in 1950. These are precinct figures, not village counts. Mass refrigerated household AC cannot explain the beginning of that much earlier sequence. This timing argument does not deny early passive cooling or later evaporative equipment.

The book should also avoid calling Santa Rosa an uninterrupted growth success. Its more durable concentration of services can remain the comparison even when its own population stagnates or falls. Survival of a regional service centre and continuous population growth are different outcomes.

## 3. Housing could affect adoption

The 1980 report provides useful context from the same table and housing universe:

| Area | Housing built 1970-March 1980 | Built 1939 or earlier | Public/company water | Public sewer |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Santa Rosa | 18.8% | 32.3% | 97.6% | 93.1% |
| Guadalupe County | 16.2% | 39.4% | 80.2% | 65.1% |
| Los Lunas | 60.3% | 13.8% | 92.5% | 81.0% |

Santa Rosa had a much smaller share of recently built housing than Los Lunas, despite their similar AC percentages. Equipment adoption and large-scale residential construction were not the same process. This supports keeping cooling, housing supply and access to work separate in the analysis.

These figures do not establish the costs of fitting any particular Cuervo house. Public-water coverage is not an electric-grid measure. Central heating is not synonymous with central cooling or even compatible ductwork. Housing construction can also respond to growth, so it cannot simply be inserted as an independent cause.

The appropriate economic question is the **total cost of obtaining comfort**: equipment, installation, the building and utilities. Buying the same appliance in the same region need not give every household the same cost. Conversely, household differences alone do not establish a town-wide cooling disadvantage.

## 4. Electricity: one historical anchor, one unresolved comparison

An unexpected primary lead is *In re United Power Co. Taxes for 1937*, 44 N.M. 542, 105 P.2d 741 (New Mexico Supreme Court, September 16, 1940). Its opening account of the pleadings describes Santa Rosa's municipal electric-light and water plants, service to inhabitants, and a lease arrangement involving United Power. This is documentary evidence of municipal utility infrastructure in the 1937 tax dispute. The ruling concerns procedure; it is not a household service survey. [Opinion transcription](https://calculators.law/caselaw/decisions/oeW8n71z53dM/in-re-united-power-co-taxes-for-1937).

Farmers' Electric's own site dates its formation to 1938 and lists Guadalupe among the counties it currently serves. That does not date the arrival of power in Cuervo or identify every historic service territory. It must not be confused with similarly named cooperatives elsewhere. [New Mexico cooperative](https://www.fecnm.org/).

Targeted web searches and a search of held Clipper OCR did not establish Cuervo's first household connections, historical tariffs, reliability, or an installation-cost difference against Santa Rosa. The OCR proximity hits inspected were unrelated material and personal names such as Powers. This negative search is not evidence that Cuervo lacked electricity. The early newspaper holdings also cannot settle a later rural-electrification date.

**Result:** equal historical access remains unverified. So does an alleged Cuervo disadvantage. Neither belongs in the manuscript as a fact.

## 5. Climate: cooling was relevant, but the two-town test is unavailable

NOAA's GHCN-Daily inventory identifies a Cuervo station, **USC00292281**, at 35.0333 N, 104.4167 W, elevation 1,476.1 metres. Its inventory lists precipitation and snow observations in 1948-1952, but **no TMAX or TMIN series**. Finding a Cuervo station therefore does not supply the temperatures needed for a historical cooling comparison.

Santa Rosa station **USC00298107** has maximum and minimum temperature inventory coverage from 1918 to 2020, with gaps. The station listing places it at 34.9561 N, 104.7217 W, elevation 1,417.3 metres. These are archive station coordinates, not proof of unchanged instrument locations throughout the record. [Station inventory](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/ghcn/daily/ghcnd-stations.txt); [element coverage](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/ghcn/daily/ghcnd-inventory.txt).

For physical context, I calculated July averages from the Santa Rosa daily record:

| Period | Mean daily July high | Mean daily July low | Qualifying Julys |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1931-1960 | 91.0 F | 63.6 F | 29 |
| 1961-1990 | 93.0 F | 63.0 F | 28 |

Method: retain nonmissing observations with blank NOAA quality flags; require at least 28 valid days per July and temperature element; average the qualifying monthly means with equal year weights. The omitted years are 1956, then 1970 and 1978. These are our descriptive calculations, **not official NOAA normals**, a homogenized climate-trend estimate, indoor temperatures, or values assigned to Cuervo. No humidity or heat-index series is inferred. [Daily source](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/ghcn/daily/all/USC00298107.dly); [reproducible calculation](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/home_cooling_2026-09-24/analyze_climate.py); [monthly observations](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/home_cooling_2026-09-24/santa_rosa_july_months.csv).

The record supports taking summer comfort seriously. It does not demonstrate a large heat-exposure difference between the towns. Nor can average overnight lows establish conditions inside a particular house.

Evaporative cooling is an essential separate category. The National Weather Service's Albuquerque office notes its continued use in New Mexico and reduced effectiveness during the monsoon. That establishes present regional relevance, not a historic Cuervo adoption rate. [NWS discussion](https://www.weather.gov/abq/prepawaremonsoonheatstress). A 1960 household classified as having no AC could still have used such a cooler, fans, shade or ventilation.

### Application to the book

The relevant framework is spatial equilibrium: people compare earnings, housing costs and living conditions across places. Cooling changes one part of that choice. The same comfort gain can have different consequences where jobs, houses and services differ. This is an interpretation of the case, not an estimated model.

Three effects remain conceptually possible:

1. **Regional attraction:** cooling makes the wider area more tolerable; established centres receive more of any new demand.
2. **Residential retention:** cooling helps existing households remain, including households whose work and shopping take them elsewhere.
3. **Unequal adoption:** income, utilities or housing make comfort cheaper or more accessible in one place.

The direction of a Cuervo effect cannot be selected from theory alone. Cooling might favour concentration, persistence outside town, or both. It does not itself create a rail shipment, school enrollment, merchant margin or reason for a business to occupy a particular location. Those still require the book's evidence on local functions and their customers.

Commercial and automotive cooling are separate channels. This sweep concerns the reader's household question; evidence about shops or cars cannot silently substitute for it.

## 7. Decisions and remaining limits

| Proposed claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Mass household refrigerated AC explains the beginning of Cuervo's decline | Poor chronological fit with the earlier commercial and population evidence |
| Cooling became common in Santa Rosa | Supported by its reported 1980 housing figure |
| Cooling alone ensured local population growth | Inconsistent with the observed coexistence of substantial uptake and stagnation/decline; no claim that the unobserved no-cooling outcome would be the same |
| It cost the same to install and operate cooling in Cuervo and Santa Rosa | Plausible conditional premise, not established historically |
| Cuervo lacked electricity or AC while Santa Rosa had them | Not established |
| Cooling had a negligible total effect on Cuervo | Not established |
| Cooling improved comfort without sustaining a full set of town services | Plausible mechanism consistent with the case, not a measured causal result |

**Editorial decision:** keep a short, concrete passage in Chapter 1 and this fuller assessment behind it. Chapter 7 can distinguish residential comfort from the economic demand sustaining town services. The evidence warrants neither a new AC-centred explanation nor an assertion that AC was irrelevant.

No further archive collection is necessary to make that bounded argument. A stronger causal claim would require a dated local difference in equipment, cost or service, plus evidence linking that difference to household decisions. Even a date for the first electrical connection would not, on its own, supply the latter link.
