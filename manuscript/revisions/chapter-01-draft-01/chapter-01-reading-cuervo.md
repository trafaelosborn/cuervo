# Chapter 1
# Reading Cuervo

<!-- DRAFT 1 | 2026-09-24. Main chapter begins below. Editorial status and author-review items are in chapter-01-editorial-notes.md. -->

On a visit to Cuervo, my dogs played with the dogs of people who still ranched nearby. It is a small detail with which to begin a history of a town, but it complicates the word usually used to describe this one: ghost.[^1]

The people had work there. Their dogs had a home. The surrounding country belonged to a present that a visitor could easily overlook while looking at what remained of the past. Cuervo had lost a great deal, but the encounter made the question of what it had lost more interesting. An account of the place would have to accommodate people whose connection to it continued.

On a visit I was also told that local families maintained the church. The account I heard connected the reduction of priestly visits with the small number of people left to attend. It was a conversation with a woman I met, not a parish history, and it supplied neither the date nor the circumstances of a decision. What stayed with me was the distinction it suggested: the work of caring for a place could continue after the arrangements that once brought people together there had changed.[^1]

There is documentary evidence for parts of that continuing life. A 2017 funeral notice named Dorothy Chavez's involvement in the restoration of Santo Niño Church. A 2023 notice announced another funeral Mass there. Neither tells us how often ordinary services took place. Together they give the church a more particular history than an empty building in a departed town. Restoration, occasional worship and a regular service schedule are different forms of use, sustained in different ways.[^2]

Ranching is more than a remembered encounter, too. In a January 2026 interview, Jim Armendariz described his father's purchase of a ranch outside Cuervo in 2020 and an operation using grazing leases in several places. That July, the Sand County Foundation named Rancho Armendariz of Cuervo a finalist for the New Mexico Leopold Conservation Award. These are accounts of a working enterprise. They do not connect it to the people I met, or turn one family's recent purchase into a record of unchanged ownership across a century. They do establish that livestock production belongs to Cuervo's present.[^3]

This book begins with that coexistence. A settlement can lose shops, services and residents while the land around it remains part of a productive economy. The losses may be profound. They can make everyday life more difficult, remove reasons for people to gather and leave a built landscape that seems to have outlived its purpose. Yet the activities that remain, and the places to which other activities have moved, are part of the same history.

To understand Cuervo, we have to recover what people once found useful about coming here.

## The visible explanation

The highway offers an immediate explanation. Route 66 and Interstate 40 give the visitor a familiar sequence: the old road brought trade; the new road carried it away. The interstate's physical presence makes that account easy to see. Its scale can stand for everything that happened to the much smaller place beside and around it.

Versions of this story appear in preservation writing, travel accounts and Route 66 histories aimed at visitors. The National Trust for Historic Preservation's account of Cuervo identifies interstate construction as the beginning of decline. Other writers give it the role of a decisive later blow. The distinction matters. Jim Winnerman's 2022 account in *Wild West*, for example, acknowledges earlier railway disappointment and says that most residents had already left before the interstate arrived. The published story is varied enough that it deserves to be read as carefully as the town itself.[^4]

There is a real historical question behind the familiar explanation. Moving traffic can change who passes a business, how easily a driver can stop and which premises retain a useful position. A hotel, a filling station and a store serving surrounding ranches may experience the same road alteration differently. An interchange provides a connection, but its existence alone tells us little about the trade available at a particular door. Conversely, a road's imposing presence does not reveal how many customers a business lost.

The challenge is to relate those possibilities to Cuervo's actual sequence. The history had been unfolding long before the interstate project of the 1960s, and before Route 66 received its designation in 1926. Merchandise and livestock moved through Cuervo. Families came for school. Proprietors invested, withdrew and tried again. The highway entered a place already made by other connections and decisions.[^5]

Starting the explanation with the last large construction project reverses the order in which people experienced the town. It invites us to judge a school by its eventual disappearance before asking why families wanted it, or a merchant by the later condition of a building before asking what the business did. It also makes every early event look like a preliminary stage of an ending that the participants could not know.

The people reading a local newspaper in 1911 did not know the Cuervo that would later be photographed. They had a school to build, goods to obtain and journeys to make. Their decisions concerned the usefulness of the place as they knew it and the prospects they could imagine. Those prospects could differ from one enterprise to another. Recovering them gives the history its movement: the future remained open even when some ventures were already in difficulty.

The interstate belongs in this book because transport mattered. The question is how it mattered, to which activities, and when. Answering it requires a history long enough to include the reasons for gathering here in the first place.

## Several futures in one newspaper

The September 14, 1911, *Cuervo Clipper* offers a useful place to begin that longer history. It reported that Big Jo Lumber was shipping out its stock and reducing prices on what remained. In the same issue, a contractor was erecting a new school, while the railway's section foreman had widened and improved the depot platform. Commercial withdrawal, educational investment and work on railway facilities occupied the same front page.[^6]

Each item offers a different direction of travel. Stock was leaving one business. A new building was taking shape for another purpose. A transport facility was receiving attention. The paper gives no warrant for balancing these changes into a single measure of prosperity. It gives something more useful for understanding the people involved: evidence that the prospects of different activities could diverge within the same small place.

The school was a response to an existing demand. In September 1910, the paper reported sixty-seven pupils and said that families were moving into Cuervo from the surrounding country so their children could attend. Its expectation of a hundred pupils was a forecast; the sixty-seven were the reported enrollment. By April 1912 the paper described a new two-story school, two teachers and another round of school-related moves.[^7]

These reports suggest a settlement whose importance exceeded the number of people permanently living beside its buildings. Families had reasons to arrange part of their lives around Cuervo. The school's presence helped explain their arrival. A population figure, taken by itself, would reverse part of that relationship if it treated the people as simply a stock of customers that had appeared first and caused the institution to follow.

This was also country in which merchants handled more than household shopping. In March 1910, the *Clipper* reported fourteen cars of sheep loaded at Cuervo for Kansas. In the following week's issue, Bond & Wiest was unloading separate cars of flour, sugar and grain. The firm dealt in merchandise, credit, sheep and wool. Outgoing agricultural goods and incoming supplies made Cuervo a point of connection between dispersed producers and more distant markets.[^8]

The firm's history supplies an important counterweight to the apparent fragility of the town. Frank H. Grubbs, writing in 1958, reconstructed its earlier finances from business records. His table reports positive company net profits in each of the twelve years from 1904 through 1915, including a recovery after a trough in 1910 and 1911. The figures are historical accounts, not a measure of every resident's well-being. They nevertheless describe a functioning enterprise with a more complicated trajectory than uninterrupted deterioration. Grubbs also described the Cuervo business as still operating when he wrote.[^9]

That longevity changes the scale of the story. Some arrangements could last for decades while others proved harder to maintain. Cuervo's history includes durable work as well as brief enterprises, and opportunities as well as disappointments. A chronology assembled only from departures would obscure the reasons people continued to trade and live there.

The newspaper itself eventually supplied a particularly revealing account of withdrawal. On December 1, 1922, Katharine A. Hicks announced that she was ending her financial connection with the *Clipper*. She described substantial losses and argued that the county could support one first-class weekly, preferably at the county seat. Her associate Charles Gerhardt planned improvements to his Santa Rosa paper, the *Public Voice*.[^10]

Hicks also explained that the purchase of the *Clipper* had served political purposes. The paper had returned to Democratic alignment and helped the party's county and state candidates. She regarded those purposes as achieved and the financial loss as a campaign contribution. Her assessment of the newspaper's market was therefore part of a decision made by an owner with several objectives. The financial results mattered; so did the reasons she had been willing to sustain them.

This was an argument for concentrating one kind of activity elsewhere, made before Route 66. It does not date the paper's final issue or pronounce judgment on the whole settlement. It tells us why one participant wanted a particular arrangement to change. Read beside the merchant's persistence and the school's earlier attraction, it suggests a history in which the practical basis of each activity deserves attention.

## Where the town ends

The name Cuervo can refer to more than the cluster of buildings that first attracts the visitor. It also appears in records that describe larger territories, networks and administrative relationships. A town's history can become misleading when the familiar name conceals a change in what is being counted.

The census makes the problem concrete. Published figures for Cuervo election precinct 6 record 287 people in 1930, 188 in 1940 and 210 in 1950. The fall and subsequent increase concern the precinct, which extended beyond the built settlement. They are not a sequence of head counts taken only among the houses a visitor sees. Earlier census comparisons introduce further complications because the reporting geography changed.[^11]

Even with that distinction retained, these observations complicate a simple account of uninterrupted growth until the interstate. The reported precinct population was substantially smaller in 1940 than in 1930. It then increased by twenty-two over the following decade. Three observations give the movement a shape, but they leave much of the intervening life invisible. Births, deaths, arrivals and departures could combine in different ways to produce the same total.

At this scale, a few households can make a conspicuous difference. A rise on a graph need not mark a general revival of every business, just as a fall does not identify the service whose loss caused it. The census counts people at particular moments. The newspaper reports events that seemed worth printing. Business accounts record another set of relationships. Their value grows when they are brought into conversation without being made to answer identical questions.

A person's geography can be equally difficult to reduce to one location. John Higgins Jr.'s family account records graduation from Santa Rosa High School in 1969 and a family move from Cuervo to Santa Rosa in 2006. The thirty-seven years between those dates are enough to show why attending school elsewhere cannot automatically be treated as leaving home. His wider work history requires its own attention; the two dates do not prove uninterrupted residence between them. They do identify different kinds of connection.[^12]

For the same reason, a place can lose the local provision of a service while people associated with it continue to obtain that service elsewhere. The journey, the expense and the change in everyday encounters may all matter. A school destination, shopping trip or administrative address describes a relationship. A residential move describes another. The evidence becomes more informative when those relationships remain distinct.

Railway records provide a striking example. Rock Island's December 1974 station book listed Cuervo without an agent and assigned its billing to Tucumcari. Santa Rosa had the same billing assignment. For this particular purpose, the larger neighboring town and Cuervo both belonged to an arrangement centered farther east.[^13]

The entry does not record a shipment or tell us where a rancher bought groceries. It does make the region harder to picture as a contest between two self-contained settlements. One place could serve as a center for one activity and rely on another for something else. Cuervo's relationships therefore have to be followed by function, allowing their geography to change.

The question of where Cuervo ends has no single answer useful for every part of this history. A school drew families from surrounding country. A merchant dealt with buyers far away. A precinct contained people beyond the village. A railway account belonged within a larger administrative system. The settlement was a place where some of these relationships met. Its boundaries on the ground were never the limits of its economic life.

## The work of making a town

Once those connections become visible, the opening of a business requires explanation as much as its closure. A productive countryside does not determine in advance that a merchant will locate at one particular site, that a teacher will be available there, or that people will maintain a newspaper. People have to organize those activities, find support for them and keep them going.

The early merchant's usefulness is comparatively tangible. Grubbs traces the Cuervo operation to a predecessor at Cabra in 1899 and a relocation around the beginning of the twentieth century. His account connects that move with railway access. The business combined several kinds of dealing: it sold supplies, extended credit and participated in livestock and wool transactions. Its purpose becomes clearer when we look outward from the premises to the producers and markets it connected.[^9]

Economics provides a language for some of these relationships. Adam Smith linked the division of labor to the extent of the market. A specialized activity needs sufficient opportunity to make the specialization worthwhile. In Cuervo's case, the relevant market could include people living well beyond the village, while one firm combined work that might otherwise have required several separate businesses. The arrangement is documented; the precise savings it produced are not.[^14]

Central-place theory likewise directs attention to places supplying goods and services to surrounding populations. Different activities can require different customer bases and forms of support. A merchant, a newspaper and a school do not become viable simply because the same number of people live nearby. Their costs, purposes and ways of obtaining income differ. The *Clipper* makes those differences historically specific when it records school-related arrivals, a proprietor's withdrawal or attempts to sustain a service.

These concepts help explain why a rising price for cattle need not produce a new shop on Cuervo's streets. Income from production can support many kinds of work, in many locations. The question is which transactions and jobs gather in the settlement, and what arrangements make it useful for them to occur there. Douglass North's writing on agriculture and regional growth makes the relationship between exports and subsequent development conditional on how production and income are organized. Agricultural activity alone cannot stand in for a history of the town.[^14]

The same reasoning leaves room for more than one outcome when connections improve. Better access might enlarge a local merchant's reach, make supplies easier to obtain or give customers more attractive alternatives elsewhere. The effect depends on the activity and the circumstances. A road or railway is a connection whose consequences have to be followed through the uses people make of it.

For Cuervo, the evidence is strongest when it shows those uses and arrangements directly. It is weaker when asked to allocate a precise share of the later contraction to every change in transport, agriculture or institutional provision. The distinction shapes the explanation rather than preventing one. The history can identify the work that gathered here, show that its requirements differed, and trace substantial losses and reorganizations without assigning every departure to a single preceding event.

Nor does understanding the town require finding a universal population below which recovery became impossible. A service can depend on one essential worker, on reliable support or on premises suited to its use. Several services can help make a place attractive together. These dependencies make a small settlement vulnerable, but they do not supply a common numerical threshold. The historical question concerns the arrangements Cuervo actually had and the alternatives available as those arrangements changed.

That question also restores people to an account that could otherwise be told entirely through infrastructure and population curves. Hicks decided which purposes justified supporting a paper. Families arranged residence around schooling. Merchants connected their customers with distant goods and buyers. Their actions took place within wider economic conditions, but the conditions became a town through particular decisions and continuing work.

## Reading the remains

Photographs make part of this history unusually immediate. A wall, a doorway or an exposed room can bring the viewer close to the material setting of former activity. The camera records a particular condition with a specificity that a population table cannot offer. It also frames that condition, leaving almost everything beyond the image unseen.

The photographs accompanying this book need to be read with both qualities in mind. They can show surviving fabric, damage and the relation of a structure to its surroundings. They cannot by themselves identify a former occupant, date a departure or explain a collapse. Nor can an accumulation of pictures of unused buildings establish that every part of the surrounding economy has stopped. A photographic sequence makes an argument through selection as well as through its captions.

That is one reason to bring the photographs into contact with records of activity. The school reports give arrivals and investment a place beside the images of loss. The merchant's history extends the field of view beyond a single premises. Accounts of contemporary ranching give the surrounding country a present tense. The sources do different work, and their combination should make the place more intelligible without making one source illustrate more than it can show.

There are scholarly precedents for this way of looking. Studies of former mining communities have distinguished an outsider's impression of dereliction from the meanings a place retains for its residents. Rural geographers have examined different degrees of abandonment and reuse, including declining settlements surrounded by continuing agriculture. Those observations help locate Cuervo within a broader history of uneven change.[^15]

Cuervo's contribution lies in the particulars we can recover: the trade that helped assemble a service center, the institutions that drew people into it, the different forms of support its activities required, and the connections that survived or changed as its built concentration diminished. The small scale brings some of those arrangements into view. It also requires care about how much can be inferred from a single notice, household or enterprise.

The chapters that follow begin with formation and the business of the surrounding country. They turn to the work of maintaining services, the lives that crossed between places, and the changing uses of railway and road. Together those histories make it possible to examine the cumulative contraction of the settlement while keeping its continuing relationships in view. The argument emerges from their sequence, including the episodes of recovery and persistence that make an easy ending difficult.

There is no need to deny the losses visible at Cuervo. They are the reason for asking how the place worked. But the surviving buildings are only part of what needs to be explained. So are the families who came for school, the merchant who handled wool and credit, the publisher who chose to put her support elsewhere, and the people whose work still brings them into the surrounding country.

Before asking how Cuervo came to look as it does, we need to understand what made it a place to come to.
