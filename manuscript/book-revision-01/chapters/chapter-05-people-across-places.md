# Chapter 5
# People across places

John Higgins Jr. graduated from Santa Rosa High School in 1969. His family account dates a move from Cuervo to Santa Rosa to 2006. Thirty-seven years separate the two events. His education in the larger town had preceded that residential move by most of a working lifetime.[^1]

The same account describes work on several ranches in New Mexico and Oklahoma, as well as the family ranch at Cuervo. It does not establish uninterrupted residence between graduation and the move. Its value lies in the distinction it does establish. Attending school in Santa Rosa and moving a home there were separate events, with separate dates.

That distinction changes the question asked of Cuervo's population. The loss of a local service could change a household's destinations before it changed its address. A pupil could attend school elsewhere. A ranch worker could use the town for supplies. A resident could hold a job beyond the place in which the census counted the household. Those connections helped determine what the settlement provided and what it obtained from outside.

Population counts describe the people enumerated within a reporting area. They are indispensable evidence of change, but they cannot by themselves identify these arrangements. For that, the totals need to be opened up: into ages, households, occupations and selected histories with dates attached. The resulting picture is a community whose composition changed as well as its size.

## The population inside the name

The published census records 545 people in Cuervo precinct in 1910, 438 in 1920, 287 in 1930 and 188 in 1940. In 1950, the count rose to 210. This is the basic early demographic sequence: successive smaller reported populations, followed by a modest increase at the last of these five observations.[^2]

The word *precinct* is essential. These counts cover an administrative area that included the settlement and surrounding ranch country. They are not a census of the buildings visible beside the highway. A family living some distance from the commercial center could belong to the population reported under Cuervo's name.

The geographic distinction suits the economy described in the preceding chapters. Merchants served a territory, and schools drew children from outside the village. Some customers and pupils lived within the wider precinct. Others could cross its boundary. The census unit supplies a defined population for a particular enumeration; it does not automatically supply the complete market for every institution.

The territory also needs attention over time. The 1920 publication withheld earlier precinct comparisons because of redistricting. County boundaries had changed as well. The difference between 545 in 1910 and 438 in 1920 therefore cannot be assigned wholly to births, deaths and migration within an unchanged Cuervo area. It combines reported populations whose exact territorial correspondence has not been reconstructed.[^3]

This is a reason to handle the sequence carefully, rather than discard it. The later tables report Cuervo at 287 in 1930, 188 in 1940 and 210 in 1950. The contraction between the first two observations was substantial: ninety-nine people, or about a third of the earlier count. The following increase recovered twenty-two. The reported population remained much smaller than it had been in 1930.

The original household schedules add a useful check. Counting the named rows within the identified Cuervo pages reproduces all five published totals. In 1910 and 1920, the enumeration districts also included Colonias. The handwritten locality headings identify where Cuervo's pages end. Counting every page under the district number would mix the two places.[^4]

This reconstruction gives 1,668 appearances across five censuses. Some people appear more than once, so the number is not a count of distinct individuals. The schedules provide repeated views of the population at ten-year intervals. Linking particular people across those views requires more than assuming that a familiar surname always belongs to the same family.

Nor does the absence of an earlier Cuervo category establish an empty landscape. The 1900 records use different precinct and locality labels. Rural households could precede the town's commercial concentration and later appear under its name. The beginning of a named statistical series need not be the beginning of settlement on the land.[^3]

Once these boundaries are kept in view, the early counts establish the scale of the problem. Cuervo's decline began well before the interstate era. Yet the people counted in the smaller community were not simply a residual population waiting to disappear. Their ages and households show a more active process.

## Smaller did not mean older

In 1940, at least ninety of Cuervo precinct's 188 recorded residents were under eighteen. One age remains unread, so the count could be ninety-one. Nearly half the population consisted of children. The median age was eighteen.[^5]

The preceding censuses do not reveal steady aging. Allowing for the unread entries, the median was between twenty and twenty-one in 1910, twenty-one in 1920, and between eighteen and nineteen in 1930. The population recorded under Cuervo's name became smaller while remaining young. Its median age in 1940 was below the earlier values.

The distinction between a number and a share matters here. The known under-eighteen count fell from 241 in 1910 to ninety in 1940. There were far fewer children to teach or raise. But children remained a large part of the population: roughly forty-four percent at the earlier observation and forty-eight percent at the later one. A locality could lose much of its population without becoming predominantly elderly.

<!-- FIGURE: population-and-age -->

These observations rule out a simple account in which earlier decline had already left Cuervo with almost nobody young enough to work or form a family. They do not establish why the age distribution took this shape. Births, deaths and movement all mattered. So did the aging of people who remained. A census age profile records their combined result.

The 1950 observation brings a change. Its median age was 25.5, while the under-eighteen count was eighty-five, or about forty percent of the population. Cuervo was larger than in 1940 and older at the same time. The twenty-two-person gain therefore did not consist of a corresponding increase in children.

These are calculations from handwritten schedules, not published precision to the last decimal place. Seven ages remain unresolved across the five enumerations. The ranges in the figure show how those unread values could affect the median. They do not cover every possible reporting or transcription error. Even with that limitation, the broad finding is secure enough to guide the narrative: contraction and aging did not move together throughout the early history.

The wartime question needs the same care. The 1940 schedule contains thirty-six people recorded as male aged eighteen to forty-four, plus fourteen boys aged twelve to seventeen. These groups are demographic descriptions, not reconstructed draft eligibility. Some adolescents would reach military-service ages during the war. Calling the whole population too young to serve would overlook them.[^6]

The 1950 supplementary questions also contain five affirmative answers for service during the Second World War. They include a probable match for Henry Yates, who had appeared in his parents' Cuervo household in 1940. Those answers show that veterans were present in the later population. They cannot count everyone who served from Cuervo, including people who died or were living elsewhere by 1950.[^7]

A census taken before the war and another taken after it leave most wartime changes between their observations. Temporary employment, enlistment, return, marriage and relocation could occur within that interval. The schedules provide starting points for those histories. Their age distributions alone cannot explain the net population change or establish a difference in military losses relative to Santa Rosa.

## A rebound made of households

The increase from 188 to 210 was 11.7 percent. In a large city, that percentage would imply thousands of additional residents. In Cuervo precinct, it amounted to twenty-two people. Both descriptions are correct. The percentage measures change relative to a small starting population; the head count shows how few family decisions could affect it.

The household reconstruction reveals a larger change in living arrangements. The 1940 schedules contain forty-two groups organized around a listed household head. The 1950 schedules contain fifty-nine. Their average size fell from about 4.48 people to 3.56. The population increased modestly while the number of recorded groups rose by roughly forty percent.[^8]

These groups were reconstructed from the head entries and their associated members. They are not a count of newly built houses. Two separately headed groups could share a dwelling, and a group could include relatives or other residents beyond a married couple and children. The reconstruction follows the people whom the schedule placed together.

The increase was particularly visible among small groups. Those containing one or two people rose from thirteen to twenty-seven. Groups of three or more rose from twenty-nine to thirty-two. A picture of expanding large families alone would therefore miss the main change in how the recorded population was divided among households.

<!-- FIGURE: people-and-households -->

This could alter the demands made on the local economy. Fifty-nine households might organize shopping, cooking, heating and travel differently from forty-two larger ones. Some tasks and expenses can be shared within a household. Establishing a separate household can create new requirements even when the people involved already live in the locality. The schedules reveal the change in grouping, without measuring its effect on spending.

The children supply another check. The 1950 schedules contain fifty-one children under ten. The 1940 schedules contain fifty-two known children in that age range, with one additional unresolved age elsewhere in the population. The later increase in total residents was accompanied by a nearly unchanged number of young children.

Subtracting the fifty-one under-ten children from the 1950 total leaves 159 people aged ten or older. The cohorts that could already have been alive in 1940 were thus represented by twenty-nine fewer people than the original population of 188. The later children more than offset that difference, leaving the twenty-two-person net gain.[^9]

This arithmetic does not identify fifty-one local births and twenty-nine departures. Children could arrive from elsewhere, while earlier residents could leave or die. Adults who moved in could replace some of those losses. The calculation describes two age-defined populations at their endpoints; it cannot allocate the change among those events.

The distinction also explains why a modest rebound can conceal considerable movement. Arrivals and departures can offset one another. A stable household total can conceal changes in membership. Conversely, new household groups can form among people already present. The census count is the balance left after those changes, rather than a record of each change itself.

In a small population, such events can produce a noticeable percentage change without marking a lasting economic turn. That does not make them causeless or permit a statistical declaration that the increase was random. It means that the 1950 point needs supporting evidence before it can be called a sustained recovery. The household entries provide some of that evidence by showing what changed within the total.

## Families changed within the totals

The Yates entries show one way a population could divide into more households. James and Minnie Yates headed a six-person group in 1940. Henry, aged twenty-four, was among its members. In 1950, the parents' group contained four people, while a thirty-four-year-old Henry headed a separate three-person household. Names, ages and family relationships make this a strong candidate link.[^10]

The later arrangement contained two households where the earlier record had shown one. That does not make every member of the later groups a continuing resident from the earlier family. Marriage and other changes could introduce different people. It does show why an additional household need not represent the arrival of an entirely new family from outside the precinct.

The Trujillo household changed in another direction. Juan B. and Reyes appeared with six sons in 1940, making a group of eight. In the probable corresponding household in 1950, the couple lived with a grandson. The group had fallen to three. The six sons were absent from that household, but their absence does not identify their destinations or establish that all had left the precinct.[^10]

A change of five people within one household is large beside a net precinct gain of twenty-two. It could be offset by growth in other families without appearing as a decline in the total. The arithmetic makes the scale of household turnover visible. It supplies no common motive for the sons' absences.

Paul E. and Mildred May Bailey's household moved the other way, from nine members to twelve. The parents' recorded ages increased from forty-one and twenty-eight to fifty-one and thirty-eight. Three children were under ten in the later census. The family combination strongly supports a continuing household with additional younger members, although some children's names and ages require caution.[^10]

Other units changed less. The likely Jose Maria and Antonia Duran couple appeared as a two-person household at both observations. An unchanged size offers one kind of continuity. It cannot guarantee uninterrupted residence between the census dates, but it belongs alongside the more dramatic changes. The community contained several household trajectories at once.

Taken together, these cases explain how a small net increase could accompany substantial reorganization. A household could divide, another could lose adult children, and a third could gain younger ones. The population total would record the combined balance. Its single upward movement could sit above quite different experiences.

The large Bailey family also raises a plausible question about ranching. Agricultural households might retain or attract children for reasons connected with their livelihoods. But the record does not support assigning the entire rebound to unusually large ranch families. Under the project's occupation classification, twenty-four households in 1950 had at least one ranching entry. They contained ninety-one people, including eighteen children under ten.[^11]

Those households accounted for roughly forty-three percent of residents but thirty-five percent of the under-ten children. Large families also appeared alongside railway, construction and postal occupations. One large Chavez household had no usable main occupation entry for this classification. The distribution was broader than a simple division between prolific ranch families and everyone else.

An occupation recorded in 1950 is also an imperfect guide to the conditions under which children were born. A parent could have changed work. Adult children could have established homes elsewhere. For this book, the useful finding is narrower: households associated with ranching formed a substantial part of the population, while family growth and reorganization extended beyond them.

This keeps the agricultural explanation in proportion. Chapter 3 established an expanding county cattle economy during and after the war. That activity could support work and income around Cuervo. Household structure adds people to the account, but it does not convert the county cattle series into a local birth or migration series.

## Arrivals in a declining place

The 1950 schedules contain direct evidence that some residents had recently lived elsewhere. Supplementary questions asked selected people where they had lived one year earlier. Seven retained answers name an outside place or state. They include California, Texas, Tatum, Tucumcari and Santa Rosa.[^12]

These answers give the demographic history a timing that the ten-year comparison lacks. They place some movement close to the 1950 enumeration and after the war. The population observed in 1950 was receiving people, even though its longer history was one of contraction. Decline had not closed the locality to arrivals.

The work entries make several of those arrivals economically relevant. Mike Wood, whose earlier residence was recorded as California, was listed as a ranch hired hand. The three Simpson households containing prior-residence answers had ranching entries. Nicholas Aragon, whose earlier residence was Tucumcari, had a railway occupation. These are associations between recent residence changes and work recorded at the later date.

The schedules do not state that employment caused each move. Someone might move with a spouse, return to relatives or take a job after arriving. A person's recorded occupation and earlier address therefore identify a connection to investigate, rather than a completed explanation. Even so, these entries give the cattle and railway hypotheses a firmer basis than a resemblance between two curves.

Esther Lack's answer is particularly useful for the regional argument. Her earlier residence was recorded as Santa Rosa. She was being counted in Cuervo precinct in 1950. This is evidence of movement in the direction that a simple absorption story tends to omit. The smaller locality could receive someone from the larger center.

One such observation does not make Cuervo a growing destination for Santa Rosa households. It does establish that movement was not uniformly one-way. The economic relationship between the places has to accommodate return, arrival and continued residence as well as departure.

The six answers naming places outside Guadalupe County occurred in five households containing twenty-three people in 1950. Including Esther Lack adds another household and brings the combined membership to twenty-five. Those figures illustrate scale: a handful of households contained more people than the precinct's net ten-year gain.

They are not twenty-five proven migrants. The residence question was asked of selected people, and an answer about one member cannot automatically be extended to everyone sharing a home. Some checkboxes were overwritten or blank even where an earlier place was legible. The retained observations are useful examples, not a complete migration count to be multiplied into a precinct estimate.

## Several kinds of work under one roof

The occupation inventory places ranching near the center of Cuervo's 1950 population. Twenty-eight of fifty-six written main occupation entries fall in agriculture and ranching. Nine more concern railway work. Together, the two categories account for about two-thirds of the inventory.[^13]

The unit being counted is an occupation appearance in a schedule. It is not necessarily a person currently at work: two of the railway entries concern people looking for work. Nor does a resident's entry place the job inside the village. The figures describe the work associated with people counted in the precinct, including its rural territory.

The remaining entries broaden the picture. They include retail, food service, education, public administration, construction and other activities. Some of these workers could depend on demand generated by ranching or transport. Others served functions whose organization and funding differed from ordinary commercial purchases. The population contained several ways of earning a living within a small overall count.

The Higgins household makes that mixture concrete. In 1950, J. F. Higgins was recorded in ranch work. His son John R. was a veterans instructor, with government entered as the worker class. The father and son belonged to the same enumerated household, alongside their wives. The instructor's institution and workplace were not named.[^14]

The household therefore connected agricultural and public work without requiring the two activities to occupy the same site. It cannot supply a family budget or prove that every member pooled earnings. It does show why assigning one economic identity to an entire household can conceal useful differences between its members.

Santa Rosa's schedules contain an even more explicit combination. One household listed the father in Southern Pacific railway work and his wife as proprietor of the 66 Cafe. Two daughters had cafe occupations marked as unpaid family work. Another daughter had paid housekeeping work. Railway employment, an owner-operated business and several forms of service work appeared under one roof.[^15]

This matters to the interpretation of a transport town. The railway worker's household was also a restaurant household. A change affecting one activity could matter to relatives engaged in another. Conversely, another source of work might help a household remain. The record identifies the combination; it does not establish how much security or exposure that combination produced.

The women's entries are especially informative. Proprietorship, paid employment and unpaid work in a family business were different economic roles. Treating only the listed household head as its worker would erase the cafe's operator and several people helping to supply services. Household relationships and occupation columns need to be read together.

<!-- FIGURE: work-under-one-roof -->

Cuervo's retail entries offer another example. Stuart Holbrook was recorded as a general-merchandise manager in 1950, and his wife Blanche as a store clerk. The schedule did not name the firm. It documents two people's retail work within the household, without requiring the uncertain later biographical link to identify their employer.[^16]

The absence of a written main occupation has a different meaning. It cannot safely be counted as unemployment or absence of useful work. Household care and other unrecorded activities contributed to how people lived. The inventory measures the entries it can read and classify, rather than the total effort required to maintain a community.

Santa Rosa's larger inventory contains 643 main occupation appearances across its three municipal enumeration districts. Motor businesses, restaurants and bars, and lodging account for 181 entries. Government administration and education account for another ninety-five. Forty-three entries concern agriculture and ranching. Agricultural work could coexist with a home in the larger town.[^13]

This is a broader occupational range than Cuervo's inventory displays. The comparison also reflects different territories: a ranching precinct is being placed beside municipal districts. Classification decisions and possible duplicate entries add smaller uncertainties. The contrast helps describe the two populations; it cannot serve as a controlled estimate of how many jobs Santa Rosa took from Cuervo.

The positive finding is that both places participated in overlapping economic activities. Ranching did not confine every worker to a rural address. Railway work did not exclude a family connection to catering or public employment. These combinations supplied several possible links between a household and the wider region. The question of where that household lived remained a separate part of its history.

## School, work and home had different dates

The later Higgins accounts extend the distinction beyond one census. Willa Higgins's obituary dates her and John Sr.'s Cuervo home to 1949-1979. Their presence in the 1950 household supplies a contemporary observation within that reported interval. John Sr.'s later account describes ranching northeast of Cuervo and dates a family home in Fort Sumner to 1983.[^17]

These sources describe a continuing connection with the Cuervo area across decades of service change. They also leave a gap between 1979 and 1983. That gap need not be filled to make the relevant point: the family's residential history has dates of its own. It cannot be reconstructed simply by substituting the closure dates of local institutions.

Willa's account adds a business in another place. She owned and operated a beauty shop in Santa Rosa for many years. The operating years are not given, so an overlap with the dated Cuervo residence cannot be established. The record connects her life with both places while leaving the working arrangement unresolved.

It would be easy to turn those statements into a daily commuting story. Doing so would introduce a journey and a schedule that the sources have not supplied. The stronger account keeps the documented roles: Cuervo resident during a reported interval, Santa Rosa business owner during an unspecified one, and later Fort Sumner resident. Those facts already describe a life extending across several local economies.

John Sr.'s account also says that he was reared in Cuervo and schooled in Santa Rosa. The connection therefore preceded the son's 1969 graduation. Education outside the smaller settlement appears across two generations. The later high-school destination cannot automatically be attributed to one mid-century closure of the Cuervo school.

John Jr.'s reported 2006 move is particularly useful because it supplies what school records often lack: an explicit statement about a residential move. His earlier ranch employment extended beyond Cuervo, so the account should not be converted into thirty-seven years of uninterrupted local residence. It does separate a known educational event from a much later move bearing the same destination name.[^1]

Other family accounts widen the map. Roy Kenneth Bailey graduated from Santa Rosa High School in 1958. His obituary names ranch work, including Diamond A Cattle Company in Cuervo, and dates a Tucumcari home to his retirement in 1999. The dates of individual ranch jobs are unspecified. Santa Rosa schooling and a later Tucumcari home belong to different parts of the account.[^18]

Tito Vigil's account likewise names Cuervo schools and Santa Rosa High School, where he graduated in 1967. His later home was in Fort Sumner. The 1950 Cuervo schedule supplies a strong childhood identification through his parents, infant age and recorded November birth month. It does not provide a school-transfer or departure date.[^18]

These are selected surviving family narratives. They cannot measure how many residents took each route. Their value is to establish possibilities that a single-arrow story would suppress: schooling in one place, work across several, and a home established later in another. They make the geography of everyday life more complex without requiring every biography to be completed.

The economic implication is straightforward. A household's decision about residence could depend on several members' needs and opportunities. Access to a school elsewhere might make remaining possible, even as it reduced the smaller settlement's educational role. Work associated with a ranch could sustain a local home while purchases and other services were obtained outside it. The records show parts of these arrangements; the relative importance of each remains unmeasured.

Such connections also change the meaning of persistence. A household could retain a Cuervo address while participating less in its local commercial center. The land, home and family connection might endure as some daily business moved elsewhere. Continuing residence and a shrinking concentration of services could therefore occur together.

## A regional center that persisted

Santa Rosa's own population history requires more precision than a continuous upward line. Its municipal count more than doubled between 1930 and 1940, from 1,127 to 2,310. It then fell to 2,199 in 1950, while Cuervo's precinct count rose. Neighboring places could move in opposite directions in either decade.[^19]

After 1950, Santa Rosa's published count rose modestly, reaching 2,220 in 1960 and 2,485 in 1970. Guadalupe County's population fell from 6,772 to 4,969 over those twenty years. The county seat retained and added residents within a contracting county. The arithmetic does not identify the destinations of people missing from the county's later count.

Santa Rosa then lost population, reaching 2,263 in 1990. The apparent return to growth after that date needs a different explanation. By 2020, its total had reached 2,850. Yet the number of people living in households had barely changed, rising from 2,240 in 1990 to 2,261 in 2020.[^20]

The Census Bureau distinguishes household residents from people in group quarters. A household occupies a housing unit and can consist of one person, relatives or unrelated people living together. Group quarters include correctional facilities, nursing facilities, dormitories and other communal arrangements. Both categories contain residents, but they organize accommodation and demand for services differently.

Santa Rosa's group-quarters count rose from twenty-three in 1990 to 589 in 2020. That increase of 566 accounts for about ninety-six percent of the municipality's net gain of 587 residents. The largest change occurred in the 1990s: total population rose by 481, while household population rose by one.

In 2020, the census classified 585 Santa Rosa residents in correctional facilities for adults. Most of the group-quarters population therefore had a specific institutional explanation. It should not be interpreted as hundreds of additional people establishing ordinary households in the town.[^21]

Subtracting those residents does not reveal what Santa Rosa would have been without the correctional facility. Employment, purchases and other institutional effects could help support people living in households. The population tables do not estimate those effects. Their contribution is to identify the composition of the observed total.

Nor was stable household population equivalent to unchanged household organization. The number of Santa Rosa households rose from 810 in 1990 to 980 in 2020. With almost the same number of household residents spread across more units, average size fell from about 2.77 people to 2.31. As in Cuervo's earlier comparison, counting people and counting households tell different parts of the story.[^20]

Against the wider county, this stability was consequential. Between 2000 and 2020, Guadalupe County's household population fell from 4,155 to 3,855. Santa Rosa's rose by twenty over the same interval. Subtracting the city leaves a fall from 1,914 to 1,594 household residents elsewhere in the county. The remaining residential population was becoming more concentrated at the county seat, even without rapid growth in its own household population.[^20]

These figures describe reporting areas as they existed at each census. Municipal expansion can alter which residents fall inside a boundary. They also describe people, rather than incomes or standards of living. Within those limits, Santa Rosa is a case of considerable residential persistence amid regional contraction. Its role differs from both Cuervo's early losses and a rapidly expanding residential community.

## The Los Lunas comparison

Los Lunas supplies the latter contrast. Its household population rose from 5,936 in 1990 to 16,994 in 2020, an increase of about 186 percent. Santa Rosa's household population rose by less than one percent over the same period. Separating group quarters leaves the contrast intact.[^22]

The comparison distinguishes two demographic outcomes that total counts can blur. Santa Rosa sustained a household community of roughly similar size while adding a substantial institutional population. Los Lunas recorded a much larger population living in households. Those are different forms of growth and persistence, with different implications for housing and local services.

The municipal footprint of Los Lunas also expanded. Its growth cannot all be assigned to people moving into a fixed area. Nor do the totals identify where residents worked. The household series is consistent with expansion connected to a larger metropolitan labor and housing market, but it does not establish commuting as the cause of that expansion.

This matters to the proposed economic comparison. A nearby larger center could draw some activities away from a small settlement. Access to a much larger labor market could also make residence in a neighboring community attractive. Neither effect can be inferred from population alone. The relevant evidence concerns employment destinations, housing, travel and the land included within the count.

Los Lunas therefore helps distinguish the question, rather than solve it by analogy. Its observed residential expansion was much larger than Santa Rosa's. Cuervo's relationship with Santa Rosa cannot be explained simply by assigning it a smaller version of the same growth curve. The places occupied different positions within their regions, and their populations changed in different ways.

## What can be counted later

The named Cuervo precinct series does not continue neatly into the later census tables. County subdivisions changed, and the inspected 2010 and 2020 place files contain no separately named Cuervo place total. A large census division, a postal area and the built settlement each cover different territory. Substituting one for another would manufacture continuity.[^23]

Modern census blocks make a more local investigation possible, but they do not eliminate the boundary problem. Some blocks touching Cuervo extend far into the surrounding country. Adding all the people in every intersecting block can count residents outside the settlement. Dividing a block's population according to land area would assume that people are spread across it in a way the data do not establish.

A fixed study rectangle around the settlement illustrates the difficulty. It covers about 6.28 square kilometers, including some surrounding land. In 2010, blocks wholly inside it contained no published population, while all blocks touching its interior contained seventeen people. In 2020, the corresponding totals were five and twenty-seven.[^24]

The resulting ranges, zero to seventeen and five to twenty-seven, concern allocation across the chosen boundary. A zero lower value does not mean nobody lived in the rectangle. It means none of the published population was in blocks entirely contained within it. Residents of intersecting blocks could still have lived inside the window.

The ranges also overlap. They cannot establish a 2010-2020 increase, and they are not confidence intervals around a true village population. Small census counts carry further disclosure-protection and enumeration limitations. The useful result is a demonstration of how the geography constrains precision, rather than a new exact figure for Cuervo.

This prevents an attractive but unsupported graph running from the old precinct's 545 people to a modern handful. The earlier count included a larger rural population; the modern window was drawn for a different purpose. The two can inform the same history without becoming observations of one unchanged place.

The demographic evidence nevertheless establishes much more than a warning about measurement. Cuervo's earlier contraction left a young population. Its mid-century increase involved smaller household groups, family turnover and arrivals. Selected later histories show people using schools and pursuing work beyond the locality while retaining or renewing residential ties. Santa Rosa's later stability had a different composition from the expansion seen at Los Lunas.

These findings connect the institutions of the previous chapter with the transport history that follows. A place could lose a service while residents found another way to reach it. Whether that arrangement was practical depended partly on the journey: its availability, cost, frequency and destination. Roads and railways mattered through the connections they made possible, as well as through the ground they occupied.

The next chapter follows those connections. Passenger stops, freight arrangements, road alignments and roadside trade changed at different dates. The population record has already shown why those changes need to be examined separately. People could remain while the routes through which they worked, learned and obtained services were reorganized around them.
