# Chapter 8 - What remained

In July 2026, Rancho Armendariz of Cuervo appeared among the finalists for New Mexico’s Leopold Conservation Award. The announcement described cattle ranching, land restoration and a business selling meat directly to consumers. Cuervo was being named as a place of production decades after the contraction of much of its town economy.[^1]

Land was owned, surveyed and exchanged. A church retained parish connections and attracted restoration work. Families maintained relationships with several communities. These uses gave Cuervo a later history whose geography extended beyond the damaged commercial buildings.

## A working base in a wider territory

The contemporary ranching evidence identifies an operating base more precisely than a business address alone. In a January 2026 interview, Jim Armendariz said that his father bought a ranch outside Cuervo in 2020. He described grazing leases spread among several places, including the areas around Fort Sumner, Santa Rosa and Puerto de Luna. The locality was one part of a geographically dispersed operation.[^2]

The operation’s combined acreage and herd figures therefore cannot be assigned entirely to the village or historical precinct.

Reaching beyond the village remained necessary to production. Earlier merchants supplied some of those connections through credit, goods and shipping; the modern operation combined its own land, leases and market relationships.

Direct meat sales added a connection to consumers through the family’s business.[^1] Trade associated with the locality continued beyond the old storefronts.

A ranch can use substantial land while supporting relatively few nearby households. Its purchases and customers may be distant. Continued production could therefore sustain a working base without recreating the earlier concentration of town services.

## The lot grid after the businesses

The county records preserve another form of continuity. Cuervo’s streets, blocks and additions remained part of the language used to describe land long after many of its commercial functions had gone. A map could retain the town’s earlier divisions while later annotations recorded a different pattern of ownership.

The undated Cuervo parcel map combines a printed base with subsequent coloring. The base identifies railway facilities and merchant premises. Its lots and additions describe a planned geography; the later marks describe another layer of information. Reading them as a single moment would confuse the history the map preserves.[^3]

The lot grid preserved a geography of ownership after uses changed. Some planned development might never have occurred, and later owners could combine parcels or use them differently. A plat identifies land rather than counting occupied buildings.

Modern surveys make that continuing use of the grid concrete. The August 2013 land-exchange sheets describe parcels within the old townsite and additions. A July 2014 sheet places further parcels in relation to the railway and Interstate 40. The old divisions continued to organize new property arrangements.[^4]

The former school property still attracted buyers. In December 2011, the Santa Rosa Consolidated Schools board chose Flora Page’s $10,250 offer from four bids. It approved the quitclaim deed the following January, with the proceeds earmarked for the band program. Page had attended school in Cuervo and Santa Rosa, according to her later obituary.[^5] The property retained value long after teaching had ended.

The January 1983 assessor card discussed earlier illustrates the point. It classified a building as a gas station while describing poor condition and a salvage basis for valuation. The classification preserved an association with a commercial use. It did not establish continued fuel sales or date the last transaction.[^6]

## An institution with a different rhythm

Santo Niño Church adds an institutional history that cannot be reduced to either a building or a regular service schedule. Dorothy Chavez’s 2017 funeral notice named her involvement in its restoration. It also announced a funeral Mass at the Cuervo church for March 9. The notice connected a named person’s work and a planned gathering with the site.[^7]

The following year’s Archdiocese of Santa Fe directory listed Santo Niño in Cuervo among the missions of St. Rose of Lima in Santa Rosa. This placed the church within a wider parish organization.[^8]

A parish bulletin dated December 21, 2025 supplies a later observation. Its collection report for December 6–7 included an entry labeled “Cuervo Function” for $170. Another page listed Santo Niño/Cuervo among the missions.[^9]

Restoration work, an announced service, parish affiliation and a later financial entry establish continuing institutional ties. The later service schedule remains unresolved.

That is a different pattern from maintaining a daily commercial service. A shop needs enough transactions to sustain its chosen operation. A church building may receive work or contributions from people whose visits are less frequent. The support of an institution can extend beyond those living close enough to use it every week.

## Connections beyond residence

The family histories in Chapter 5 show how schooling, work and home could belong to different places.[^10] A resident could obtain services elsewhere; someone who moved could retain land, relatives or a church connection. The modern census supplies bounds for a chosen study window rather than a secure village count.[^11] Continued use is better established here through the named ranch, parcels and institutional ties than through a claim about the number of people remaining.

## The photograph and the frame

The photographs record materials and spatial relationships: the road embankment above adjoining land in Figure 8.2, the opening into damaged fabric in Figure 8.3.[^12] The sources supply dates and uses that the views alone cannot. A frame filled with vacant premises can also leave working land outside it. Read together, the images and records show the loss of a commercial concentration within a locality still being used.

## A locality still in use

“Ghost town” describes Cuervo’s visible losses more readily than its whole economy. Agricultural production, property transactions and institutional ties had requirements different from those of a daily shop or staffed school. Work on abandonment and reuse elsewhere makes the same distinction between a place’s appearance and the ways people continue to use it.[^13]

A usable building remains a resource, but reopening its former business would also require staff, finance and customers with a reason to come. The ranch and church demonstrate continued usefulness on their own terms. Neither needs to reproduce the town’s earlier concentration of services to belong to its history.

Cuervo’s surviving buildings record a real loss: fewer places to work, trade, and obtain services. Ranching, land transactions, and ties to Santo Niño record a locality still in use. These histories belong together. The country remained productive while much of the work that had gathered a town around it was gone.
