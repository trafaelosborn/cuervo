# Historical money: adopted editorial convention

**Proposed September 27; adopted and implemented September 28, 2026.** Chapters 2 and 3 now use selected equivalents and a constant-dollar financial figure. [Full calculations and input series](B:/Meridian/Cuervo/research_evidence/monetary_revision_2026-09-28/calculations.json) preserve the audit trail.

## Convention

Keep historical amounts, adding a rounded purchasing-power equivalent on the first important occurrence of a sum that the reader needs to size: initial business finance, a school bond or a salary. Use one fixed base year, **2025 dollars**, rather than the moving phrase "today's money." Repeated accounting entries should not each acquire a parenthetical.

For an argument about change across years, check the series in constant dollars and state the real change where it matters. Preserve original nominal figures in the source tables and identify the units of each figure. An inflation-adjusted level can aid comprehension without replacing historical bookkeeping categories.

The cattle-price discussion already reports real changes using a 1950-dollar series. Rebasing it to another year does not change those percentage changes. The merchant-finance series and relevant prose now have a separate real-dollar check in the September 28 proofs.

## Source and limits

[BLS purchasing-power method](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/purchasing-power-constant-dollars.htm): multiply the historical amount by the base-year price index divided by the historical-year index. CPI measures broad consumer purchasing power. It does not give the cost of reproducing a school, a modern business valuation or local ranch-input inflation.

[Minneapolis Fed historical CPI table](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/about-us/monetary-policy/inflation-calculator/consumer-price-index-1800-), accessed September 27 and rechecked September 28, 2026, provides a linked historical series on a 1967 basis. It identifies its pre-1913 observations as estimates; its 1890-1912 segment is the Albert Rees cost-of-living index. Its published index is 25 in 1899, 28 in 1910 and 1911, 30.4 in 1915, and 967.5 in 2025. These rounded published observations are sufficient for approximate reader-facing examples. The full financial revision uses this documented consistent series; indexes with different bases are not divided.

The BLS [calculator explanation](https://www.bls.gov/data/inflation_calculator_inside.htm) uses annual averages for past calendar years and a latest-month observation for the current year. A fixed past base year avoids moving the book's equivalents with each monthly release.

Amounts and dates derive from the chapter notes and held source readings. The 1899 example uses the predecessor's opening-year context; Grubbs places the initial capital in this founding context. The published CPI is identical in 1899 and 1900; the note records this dating basis.

The rounded profit observations imply growth of about **215% in nominal dollars** between 1911 and 1915, versus about **190% in consumer purchasing power** using this linked index. The recovery remains strong. The adjustment changes its measured scale without overturning the chapter's recovery finding. These are company accounting profits reconstructed by Grubbs, not town income or audited returns on capital.

## Methods sentence

> Dollar amounts retain their historical values. Selected equivalents, expressed in 2025 dollars, indicate approximate consumer purchasing power. Pre-1913 conversions use reconstructed price indexes. These equivalents do not estimate modern replacement costs or business values.

The convention is now stated in the chapter notes and the house style. Consolidate it into the book's eventual methods note. Individual parentheticals remain short, rounded and selective.
