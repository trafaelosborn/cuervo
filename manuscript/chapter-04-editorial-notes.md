## What this chapter adds

Chapter 3 examined the merchant and its productive territory. Chapter 4 asks what kept other local services available. Earlier summaries introduced several of these cases; this chapter develops their organizational differences:

1. The January 1919 loss of ninth-grade instruction following a teacher's departure, and the separate 1913 Hanson postal appointment problem, establish staffing requirements.
2. School enrollment and school-related moves show that an institution could attract people. The September 1921 meeting adds committees, voluntary help, practical subjects, governance questions and specific maintenance proposals.
3. Terry's medical proposal separates recurring support for availability from payment on individual occasions of use. A doctor's reported visit and a newly read 1922 conference schedule extend the account to provision across several places.
4. Hicks's editorial combines financial loss, a preference for a stronger county paper, a collaborator's teaching obligation and achieved political objectives. Her explanation is attributed, rather than treated as an audited efficiency calculation.
5. Garage reopenings, proposed reuse, operator transfers and a merchant's gasoline sales distinguish changing provision from permanent disappearance.
6. Smith and Hsu, Holmes and Morgan help explain specialization, combined tasks and the geography of service provision. The chapter does not estimate a survival threshold or trace a complete cascade.

The final section hands off to Chapter 5, *People across places*: changes in service destinations need separate evidence before they can be translated into changes in residence.
