# Chapter 3
# The business of ranch country

Bond & Wiest’s reported net profit fell to about $5,300 in 1911. Four years later it was about $16,700. The business had endured a sharp setback and recovered. Meanwhile, money committed to sheep had overtaken the value of merchandise on its shelves. The firm was changing within a town often described through a single trajectory of decline.[^1]

In approximate 2025 consumer purchasing power, the two profits were $180,000 and $530,000. Inflation reduces the size of the recovery, but leaves the finding intact: reported profit nearly tripled in real terms.[^2]

The accounts make Cuervo’s economic history more concrete. A ranching district could support a merchant through several related activities: supplying goods, extending credit, owning or handling livestock, buying wool and arranging shipments. Each produced different opportunities and risks. Their fortunes need not move together, even within one firm.

The firm’s changing composition sets the question for the later village: how much work and income did ranching keep at Cuervo? Answering it requires following production through the businesses that financed, supplied and marketed it.

## Stock on hand

Grubbs described a country store carrying substantial quantities of ordinary goods. Some customers bought supplies for long intervals, occasionally as much as a year. He found references to wholesale purchases of 10,000 pounds of beans and 20,000 pounds of potatoes. The volumes make sense for a firm supplying a dispersed market whose customers could not treat every purchase as a short errand.[^3]

Stock served a practical purpose. A customer visiting Cuervo could obtain goods already assembled there, rather than arrange each item from a distant supplier. The merchant committed money to hold those goods until they were wanted. That commitment made the shop useful, but also exposed the business to slow sales, changing prices and deterioration.

The store and warehouse therefore performed work across time as well as distance. Railway deliveries arrived in consignments; customers made purchases according to their own needs. Inventory bridged the two. Holding it required space, finance and judgment about what the surrounding market would buy.

Grubbs’s figures put merchandise inventory at about $8,100 in 1903 and $26,400 in 1907. By 1912 it was about $18,900, and in 1915 about $18,500. The stock carried on the books had expanded and then contracted. This is one part of the early business change, but it is not an annual sales series.[^3]

Inventory is stock held at a particular time; sales are goods sold over a period. Lower stock can accompany faster turnover or weaker trade. Sales observations are needed to distinguish those possibilities.

The available sales observations provide a separate view. Grubbs reported $44,230.32 in 1905, almost $96,000 in 1908, less than $61,000 in 1912 and about $79,000 in 1915. His figures describe an early rise, a setback and a partial recovery.[^3]

Selling more goods was not always the same as earning more from them. In the 1905 letter discussed in the previous chapter, Wiest explained that competition for trade along the Rock Island required close pricing. A merchant might retain customers or increase volume while accepting a smaller margin. That is a direct contemporary reason to keep sales and profits separate.[^4]

The railway contributed to both sides of the calculation. It helped bring supplies into Cuervo and connected the firm with a wider market. It also brought alternative sellers within reach. The commercial advantage of the station therefore depended partly on what the merchant could offer beyond physical access: available stock, credit, knowledge of customers and the combination of several services.

## Selling now, collecting later

Credit was a substantial part of that usefulness. Grubbs reported that more than half of sales were sometimes made on credit. Customers’ unpaid accounts consequently occupied a significant place in the business. At the end of 1915, his table records about $21,300 in book accounts, compared with merchandise inventory of about $18,500.[^5]

A book account represented money owed to the firm. It could arise because a customer obtained goods before paying for them. The sale and the arrival of cash were separate events. The merchant might recognize business on its books while still waiting for the money needed to pay suppliers or meet other expenses.

Agricultural receipts and expenses arrive at different times. Households need supplies and producers incur costs before the next sale. Credit bridged that interval, with repayment depending on the customer’s agreement and subsequent receipts.

For the merchant, credit was both a service and an exposure. Extending it could secure trade that an insistence on immediate cash would lose. It also tied up funds and created the possibility of nonpayment. The business had to judge whether the expected return from the relationship justified those costs.

Repeated dealings could give the merchant information about a borrower’s prospects, while livestock and produce transactions supplied additional knowledge. Combining trade and finance offered potential advantages whose contribution to profit remains unmeasured.

The movement in book accounts shows how variable this part of the business could be. Grubbs recorded about $17,200 in 1907, $6,500 in 1909, $16,600 in 1912 and $21,300 in 1915. A larger balance could reflect more credit sales, slower collection or other changes in the accounts. A smaller balance could reflect collection, reduced lending or write-offs. The amount alone does not choose among them.[^5]

For that reason, a merchant’s reported profit should never be mistaken for cash immediately available to its owners. Sales on credit could support earnings while creating a need for finance. A firm could be profitable in its accounts and still struggle to meet payments when they fell due.

A related Bond company supplies an explicit example. At Rosa, in Rio Arriba County, Grubbs reported that 89% of the firm’s $41,201.07 in 1914 sales were on credit. His account describes cash strain and a Bond-backed loan despite reported profits. Rosa was a separate place and business from Santa Rosa. The comparison illustrates a financial problem within the wider organization; it does not substitute for Cuervo’s missing cash-flow records.[^6]

Credit also changes the interpretation of a store’s relationship with its customers. The merchant could be a convenient supplier and an important creditor at the same time. A customer’s choice of where to buy might be affected by an outstanding account, an expected advance or a livestock arrangement. The market was organized through continuing relationships as well as posted prices.

That makes a general store a more substantial institution than its shopfront suggests. A closure could remove access to a familiar account and a source of working funds, as well as a place to purchase flour. Conversely, the transfer of finance to another provider could leave some merchandise trade intact. The functions could move together or separately.

Credit also distributed power. Its value to customers depended on the obligations attached to it and their access to alternatives. Evidence from the wider Bond network reveals how those relationships could be used.

## A business with several balances

Three measures trace the firm’s changing business: merchandise held for sale, investment in sheep at year end, and annual company net profit. The first two locate resources; the third records the reported return after costs and accounting adjustments.

The comparison between the first two is particularly revealing. In 1907, merchandise inventory stood at about $26,400 and sheep investment at $2,600. In 1913, the respective figures were $17,800 and $20,100. By 1915 they were $18,500 and $38,100. Sheep had become the larger recorded investment.[^7]

Grubbs’s own generalization that merchandise remained the heaviest investment throughout the period therefore needs correction. His table shows otherwise for 1913–1915. The discrepancy matters because a narrative centered on a declining store can miss resources being committed to another part of the enterprise.

The company could reduce stock on the shelves while expanding its stake in sheep. That did not necessarily produce more retail jobs, more residents or more premises in Cuervo. It did mean that a change in the merchandise business should not be treated as a complete description of the firm.

Its reported company net profits show a similarly uneven history. Grubbs supplies twelve annual observations from 1904 through 1915, all positive. The highest of those figures is about $18,700 in 1907. Profits then fell, reaching about $5,600 in 1910 and $5,300 in 1911, before recovering to $11,800 in 1912 and $16,700 in 1915.[^1]

Adjusting for inflation preserves the pattern. In 2025 purchasing power, reported profit rose by about 190% between 1911 and 1915, compared with about 215% in the original dollar figures. The chart applies the same adjustment to each year’s balances and profits. Sheep still overtook merchandise, and the recovery remains substantial.[^2]

Bond & Wiest recovered from the early setback and continued to earn money as the balance of its business changed. Grubbs reconstructed those earnings from older accounts, retaining their historical categories and rounding.

The individual business lines also contributed differently across the period. For 1912, Grubbs reported merchandise gross profit of about $3,800, sheep profit of $3,100 and wool profit of $3,200. By 1915, the corresponding entries were about $9,400, $8,400 and $1,100. Different parts of the business were moving at different speeds.[^7]

These entries retain their source labels. Merchandise gross profit precedes deductions that enter company net profit; other activities and accounting treatment also affect the final result. The figures show different contributions to the enterprise without supplying a fully reconcilable modern income statement.

Grubbs found J. S. Holbrook operating the Cuervo business in 1958.[^8] That observation extends its documented life well beyond the early accounts. Annual returns for the intervening decades remain unavailable, but the institution’s durability is clear.

## Sheep, wool and the work of an intermediary

The early enterprise cannot be understood through cattle alone. Sheep represented a substantial investment, wool a separate source of earnings, and the sale of animals another set of transactions. The same flock could connect a producer with more than one market over time. The merchant’s role could include ownership, finance, buying and marketing.

Grubbs identified Brown & Adams of Boston as important buyers of the Cuervo wool business. He also described a later shift toward Hallowell, Jones & Donald. These relationships placed a small New Mexico merchant within a market extending far beyond the railway towns nearby.[^9]

A distant buyer did not have to establish an office beside every flock to obtain wool. A local intermediary could assemble supply and maintain relationships with producers. From the other direction, producers could use the merchant’s connection to reach buyers they might find costly or difficult to deal with individually. The value of the intermediary lay in bringing those sides together.

Grubbs’s account also reveals bargaining within that relationship. He described Holbrook’s irritation at the position enjoyed by a Boston buyer and his reluctance to accept it without alternatives. The merchant was negotiating with suppliers and customers in more than one direction. Dependence on outside markets did not mean the terms of every transaction were uncontested.[^9]

Outside wool buyers brought income into the trade. Its local effect depended on the division among producers, the merchant and other costs, followed by the spending of those receipts.

The sheep business introduced further connections between the firm and the land. Grubbs recorded more than 11,000 company sheep at the end of 1915, including 8,800 on rent. The company’s interest in livestock therefore extended beyond animals physically held near the shop. Some production was organized through people caring for animals owned by the business.[^10]

Such an arrangement could bring together assets and work held by different parties. One participant might own sheep, another have access to land and provide husbandry. The agreement determined how they shared the result and the risk. Its effect depended on the terms, rather than on the simple fact that the relationship existed.

The term *partido* appears in the wider Bond record. In the 1938 case *Burguete v. G. W. Bond & Bro. Mercantile Co.*, the New Mexico Supreme Court described ranchers running the corporation’s sheep in return for a share of profits and increase. The case involved another Bond firm and two particular ranchers. It supplies independent evidence of a business system in which livestock arrangements, debts and land security were connected.[^11]

The court described a proposed $90,000 corporate loan sought in 1932 (about $2.1 million in 2025 purchasing power). The financing required assignments of security and access to grazing. The ranchers assigned leases and other interests, and agreed that their business would be handled through the corporation and with its approval while the loan remained unpaid. The arrangement reached beyond an ordinary purchase at a shop counter.

The litigation concerned a promised cancellation of the ranchers’ remaining debts once the corporate loan was repaid. The courts rejected enforcement of that promise against the company because the manager lacked authority to make it and the company had not ratified it. For this history, the relevant point is the range of relationships involved: company finance, rancher debt, land, sheep and trade formed parts of one arrangement.[^11]

That combination could matter greatly to a producer’s freedom to change suppliers or markets. It also gave the merchant reasons to remain involved in production beyond the sale of goods. The institutional connection was stronger than geographical proximity alone. A customer might continue to deal with a firm because of obligations and assets that could not be moved as easily as a shopping trip.

Economists describe these as interlinked markets: credit, inputs, production and sales are arranged together. Avishay Braverman and Joseph Stiglitz analyzed why linked contracts can emerge when incentives, information and risk matter. Their model concerns agricultural arrangements that should not be treated as identical to every New Mexico sheep contract. It nevertheless helps identify the question: what did combining these relationships make possible, and who gained control through the combination?[^12]

The contractual terms and available alternatives determined who gained from the arrangement. Company profit captures the merchant’s result; the connected obligations reveal why a producer’s choices require a separate account.

## A carload assembled in Cuervo

Freight provided another reason to combine activities. Grubbs described an arrangement under which the related Encino business sent hides to Cuervo. Wiest handled them without charge. Combining the consignments allowed the firms to ship in carload lots and obtain a freight-rate advantage.[^13]

Combining lots secured better freight terms. Cuervo earned its handling role by assembling goods into a more economical shipment. The connection’s value came from this work as well as from the track.

Cooperation also helps explain Wiest’s willingness to handle the hides without a separate charge. The freight saving benefited related firms, giving the transaction a purpose beyond a local handling receipt.

This kind of cooperation could support a small center. If several scattered producers or related businesses used one point to assemble shipments, that point acquired work disproportionate to its own production. Its market included the goods it could bring together, not only the goods originating next door.

The same function could become vulnerable if shipment methods or terms changed. A producer able to make an economical shipment through another channel might need less of the old intermediary’s service. This is a possible route through which production could continue while local handling declined. The Encino arrangement shows why combining shipments had once been useful.

The 1910 newspaper reports show the range of traffic with which this business operated. There were hides and pelts, incoming foodstuffs, wool, sheep for feeding and cattle going to distant markets. A merchant handling such a mix could benefit from several sources of demand, while also bearing the costs of understanding different products and buyers.[^14]

The variety did not guarantee that all the risks offset one another. Producers buying supplies might also be borrowers and livestock partners. A poor season or falling prices could affect several parts of the relationship together. Combining functions could make the firm useful while concentrating its exposure to the same underlying customers.

## Managers, branches and customer choice

The firm’s organization required finance of its own. Grubbs described the 1904 incorporation as giving Wiest a substantial holding alongside the Bonds. Part of the money needed to take up their shares was entered as amounts owed to the company. Accumulated profits were later applied against those balances, with distributions in 1906 and 1908 reducing and then clearing the debts.[^15]

Part of the ownership was thus financed through future earnings. Shares assigned a claim on the company; only the cash actually contributed was immediately available to buy stock or meet expenses.

The arrangement also helps explain how the enterprise could retain a manager who had not supplied the original cash. Wiest’s role developed through work and participation in profits. The business brought capital and management together, then used its earnings to support the ownership structure. Cuervo’s commercial institution was being built through financial arrangements as well as through premises and stock.

Grubbs distinguished these distributions from Wiest’s salary. He reported a monthly managerial salary of $75 beginning in 1904 (about $2,700 in 2025 purchasing power). The historical salary rose to $100 in 1905 and $125 in 1907. Salary paid for work; the profit interest rewarded ownership under the firm’s arrangement. The two streams should not be collapsed into a single estimate of the manager’s income.[^15]

Profits could consequently support the enterprise without immediately becoming household spending in Cuervo. Money might remain in the business or be credited against an owner’s debt. A modest cash distribution could coexist with a profitable year. To understand what the merchant supported locally, the use of its earnings matters as much as their amount.

Business organization also affected who needed to work in Cuervo. Grubbs described Wiest dividing his attention between Cuervo and Wagon Mound after Archie MacArthur became ill in 1911. Joe Holbrook Jr. assumed greater responsibility at Cuervo and was formally appointed assistant general manager in 1912. He was the father of J. S. Holbrook, whom Grubbs met in 1958. Management could be redistributed within the network while the Cuervo business continued.[^16]

The change illustrates both a resource and a vulnerability. A related business could provide experience and opportunities that a wholly isolated shop might lack. It could also compete for the same person’s time. The availability of a capable manager mattered independently of the number of potential customers around the store.

The possibility of selling the Cuervo business arose in the correspondence. Grubbs linked Wiest’s 1911 suggestion partly to demands on his time at Wagon Mound. Holbrook raised the subject again in 1915 amid concern about sheep and wool prospects. Bond was willing to consider a sale, but also thought the store could continue to pay as well as the other businesses. Holbrook remained.[^17]

The sale discussions expose choices within an operating business. Wiest faced competing demands on his time; Holbrook doubted parts of its prospects; Bond still saw potential returns. Continued operation followed disagreement rather than a settled verdict on the town.

Comparisons with the other branches also show that there was no single commercial formula. At Wagon Mound, Grubbs’s figures put merchandise net profit well above sheep net profit in 1912 and 1913. Sheep exceeded merchandise in the next two years. The business mix could change substantially within a related enterprise.[^18]

At Encino, accounting location complicates the physical story. Sheep handled there before 1908 were recorded at Española. In 1909, a zero in the sheep investment account coexisted with 1,434 sheep on hand that had been omitted from the inventory. An account located elsewhere did not mean that the activity happened elsewhere; an empty accounting category did not necessarily mean empty ground.[^18]

Branch accounts followed commercial arrangements as well as physical locations. Tracing where work occurred therefore requires the transactions behind the balances.

Customer choice provides a different view of the same network. Grubbs reproduced an exchange from Rosa in 1915 in which manager A. H. Long threatened to withdraw sheep from Porfirio Gallegos because he was shopping elsewhere. Bond advised against retaliation and urged an effort to win the customer’s trade.[^19]

The episode joins the lender’s or livestock owner’s position to the shopkeeper’s interest. Long evidently regarded the sheep relationship as a means of influencing where Gallegos bought goods. Bond preferred another response in this instance. The disagreement reveals a choice about how to use the firm’s position, as well as evidence that the customer had found an alternative seller.

Customer loyalty was being contested. The sheep relationship gave Long potential pressure over Gallegos; Bond preferred an effort to win the trade. Their disagreement reveals commercial power and a choice about exercising it.

## Ranch country changes its composition

The evidence now shifts from the firm’s early accounts to the wider agricultural economy in the following decades. Guadalupe County’s censuses record livestock and operations across the county, under definitions that changed over time. They establish the regional conditions facing Cuervo’s later trade.[^20]

In 1930, the county reported 15,509 cattle and calves and 99,296 sheep and lambs. In 1950, the corresponding figures were 41,276 and 56,412. Cattle had become much more numerous while sheep had declined. The change is substantial, although the two counts are not equivalent measures of output, income or grazing demand.

The wartime decade makes the divergence especially clear. Between the 1940 and 1950 inventories, cattle rose from 19,227 to 41,276, an increase of about 115%. Sheep and lambs fell from 67,765 to 56,412, a decline of about 17%. A description of the decade as a livestock boom would therefore conceal a consequential difference between its principal animals.[^20]

<!-- FIGURE: county-livestock -->

For a firm with a history of sheep ownership, wool buying and associated credit, that difference matters. More cattle could create opportunities while a shrinking sheep economy reduced others. Replacement was possible, but it was not automatic. The customers, expertise, contracts and handling required by one activity need not transfer unchanged to another.

The figures also show that sheep did not simply disappear. The 1959 inventory still recorded 44,426 sheep and lambs, alongside 31,020 cattle and calves. The sheep total was about 55% below the 1930 count. It remained a substantial regional activity. The appropriate description is contraction within a continuing economy.

Wool production adds another measure. The county reported 560,362 pounds shorn in 1939, 642,919 in 1944 and 544,477 in 1949. These production-year figures differ from a count of animals on an inventory date. They show why the amount of product entering trade must be distinguished from the stock of livestock at a particular moment.[^21]

The county series describes the market setting rather than Bond & Wiest’s share of it. Producers used different merchants and shipping points, so regional expansion could coexist with a firm’s loss of customers, or contraction with a larger share for a surviving supplier.

There is, however, a useful later observation of the merchant’s role. An El Rancho Milling advertisement in the *State Line Tribune* on January 2, 1947 listed Bond & Wiest of Cuervo as a feed dealer. An adjacent article described the Clovis mill, its railway spur and deliveries to ranchers. Cuervo’s business remained connected with the supply side of livestock production.[^22]

The advertisement places the merchant in feed distribution between the early accounts and Grubbs’s 1958 visit. Its sales volume is unknown; its role as a dealer is explicit.

The geography of that relationship is also instructive. Feed was manufactured in Clovis and offered through a dealer at Cuervo. Different places performed different parts of the work. Local availability did not require local manufacture. Conversely, the presence of a producer elsewhere could create a role for a Cuervo dealer.

## Fewer operations, different work

The county’s reported number of farms also changed. The census category includes ranching operations. It recorded 596 farms in 1930 and 297 in 1959. Reported land in farms rose from about 641,000 acres to about 1.59 million, and mean acreage increased from roughly 1,076 to 5,348 acres per farm.[^23]

Those figures are consistent with a move toward fewer, larger recorded operations. They cannot be treated as a count of mergers. Coverage and definitions changed, and land could be assigned according to the location of an operation’s headquarters even when it crossed county lines. The totals describe the enumerated agricultural economy, not a parcel-by-parcel map of the county.

The census itself makes part of the measurement change visible. Between 1954 and 1959 the reported farm count fell from 333 to 297. It attributed ten of those thirty-six fewer farms to the revised definition. That leaves a material difference, but the remainder is not a list of identified closures or departed families.[^23]

Operating scale is nevertheless relevant to the town’s prospects. A district can maintain production through fewer purchasers. Larger operations may make fewer but larger orders, organize more work internally or choose suppliers differently. The potential market for a merchant can therefore change even when livestock numbers remain substantial.

Operating scale and residence have different measures. One ranch can involve owners, family labor, hired workers and people living elsewhere. A falling farm count therefore leaves the associated change in households and jobs to be established separately.

The labor returns require particular care. The county’s estimated family-worker count was 593 in 1954 and 413 in 1959; hired workers were 292 and 121. But the observations refer to different seasonal weeks. The earlier count concerns late September and early October, while the later one concerns the week preceding enumeration, typically in November in this county. They cannot support a precise calculation of annual jobs lost.[^24]

The seasonal comparison directs attention to the organization of work. Family labor, periodic hiring and outside services support different amounts of year-round employment near a town.

Motor transport was already widespread in the later observations. The sample tables report trucks on 285 of an estimated 328 farms in 1954, and on 250 of 299 in 1959. Those proportions are about 87% and 84%. Truck ownership was already common in both years.[^24]

Widespread truck ownership expanded the options for buying and moving supplies. Ranchers could reach more distant sellers, carry their own goods or use other shipping points. The local effects depended on which journeys and commercial arrangements producers chose.

The households themselves were not confined to one source of income. In 1934, 390 county farm operators reported work away from the farm for pay or income. That was 45.8% of the 851 farms counted in 1935. The farm count had risen from 596 in 1930 before its later decline; the long-term endpoints conceal that intervening rise. Off-farm earnings were already common among operators, although their workplaces and occupations are unspecified.[^25]

Paid work away from the farm was already part of the county’s agricultural economy. The livelihood of a farm operator could reach beyond both the holding and the nearest settlement.

## Prices, purchasing power and timing

High cattle prices could improve the position of producers who had cattle to sell. The project’s statewide series shows a large increase in the 1940s. New Mexico’s average price received for beef cattle rose from $6.20 per hundred pounds of liveweight in 1939 to $19.20 in 1949. After adjustment by the national consumer price index, the increase was about 81%.[^26]

This was a substantial improvement in the consumer purchasing power of a unit sold. It is therefore reasonable to consider stronger cattle conditions among the possible supports for Cuervo’s small population rebound during that decade. The precinct count rose from 188 in 1940 to 210 in 1950. The economic context makes the increase intelligible without identifying a single cause for those twenty-two additional people.[^27]

Price is only one part of the producer’s calculation. Total receipts also depend on the quantity and weight sold. Earnings depend on expenses, including feed, labor, transport and finance. The statewide average reflects the mixture of animals sold, and the published beef-cattle series excludes calves. It cannot simply be multiplied by the county’s cattle-and-calves inventory to manufacture a local income estimate.

Nor does a rise in the selling price tell us what families did with the proceeds. A producer might buy supplies, repay debt, replace equipment, retain funds or invest in the operation. Some expenditures could support Cuervo businesses; others could support firms elsewhere. Higher receipts created purchasing power, but its destination remained a decision.

The subsequent price fall supplies a serious adverse context. From the 1951 peak to 1953, the inflation-adjusted statewide cattle price fell by about half. By 1956 it was about 52% below that peak. These are large changes even after allowing for the limits of using a consumer-price index to describe a ranching business.[^26]

The timing complicates a simple road explanation. The documented Route 66 relocation southwest of Cuervo dates to 1952, within the same broad interval as the cattle-price collapse. A business struggling then might face more than one pressure. The proximity of the dates cannot establish how much each contributed, or which enterprises were affected.[^28]

The rebound later in the price series is equally important. By 1973, the real cattle price was nearly twice its 1956 level and close to its 1951 peak. A general story of perpetually worsening cattle prices would therefore be inadequate. If a service disappeared during a slump and did not return during a recovery, something about the local business arrangement would also need to be considered.[^26]

A possible explanation lies in what customers do after a closure. They establish accounts and habits elsewhere. A later rise in their incomes can support those replacement suppliers. The conditions for reopening the original business may consequently differ from the conditions under which it once traded. This is a possible process, not a documented sequence for a named Cuervo store.

The price series supplies a changing economic context for the population observations. Connecting the two causally would require the intervening work, spending and household decisions. The useful finding here is the scale and timing of the agricultural pressures facing local businesses.

## What an export can support

The broader problem has a clear place in economic theory. Douglass North asked why an increase in a region’s export income sometimes supported a more varied economy and sometimes left it dependent on a narrow range of production. His 1959 treatment emphasized both the character of the export activity and the disposition of the income it brought in.[^29]

An export here means a sale outside the region, including elsewhere in the United States. Cuervo’s livestock and wool connections are examples of such trade. They brought the surrounding producers into markets larger than local consumption could have sustained. That connection was a source of opportunity, even when later stages of production took place elsewhere.

North’s argument directs attention to what the export requires locally. An activity may need substantial handling, storage, repair and other supporting work. It may also rely on services most efficiently supplied elsewhere. Some of its income reaches local workers and firms; some pays distant suppliers or accrues to owners who spend it in other places. The resulting town depends on this distribution of work and expenditure.

Cuervo’s early record identifies several local contributions. Bond & Wiest supplied merchandise and credit, handled products, maintained livestock interests and arranged market relationships. The warehouse and freight arrangements gave some of those transactions a reason to gather there. These were real commercial functions, with reported profits over a substantial interval.

The record also places limits on the growth expected from them. Sheep sent to Kansas for feeding supported a stage of production outside Cuervo. Wool buyers in Boston belonged to a market extending beyond the village. Feed manufactured in Clovis could be sold by a Cuervo dealer. The town participated in these chains without containing every activity in them.

This is why a productive ranching district need not produce a large town at each shipping point. The location of production is only part of the location of employment. A place might assemble substantial quantities of a commodity while retaining a modest amount of handling and commercial work. Another center might combine distribution, finance, administration and processing. The two can grow differently while serving the same broad economy.

The question is consequently more demanding than whether higher livestock prices should have produced shops. Shops and other services did exist at Cuervo. The issue is why that range of provision remained limited, how individual activities were sustained and what later allowed some of them to be supplied elsewhere. Those outcomes depend on the organization of demand as well as its total value.

The local merchant brought several functions together within a dispersed economy of production and trade. Agricultural change could alter the work gathered there while the land remained productive.

The firm itself is an important counterweight to a story of immediate failure. It weathered the early slump, changed its investment mix, appeared as a feed dealer in 1947 and remained in operation when Grubbs visited in 1958. Its eventual ending is not dated by those observations. Its longevity shows that Cuervo retained a useful commercial role long after the first expressions of doubt.

Other institutions required different support. The merchant could combine trade and finance across a wide territory; a school needed teachers, a physician dependable income, and a newspaper a willing backer. Those differences explain why the institutions of one town could have such different fortunes.
