# Chapter 1
# Reading Cuervo

In September 1911, a new school was going up in Cuervo. At the same time, Big Jo Lumber was shipping stock out of town and cutting prices on what remained. The railway had also widened and improved the depot platform. All three developments appeared on the front page of the *Cuervo Clipper*.[^1]

Cuervo was expanding and contracting at once. The school drew investment while the lumber firm withdrew stock; the improved depot served a place whose enterprises faced different prospects.

Today, Cuervo is commonly described as a Route 66 ghost town. Its surviving buildings help explain the label. The surrounding land, however, still supports ranching. In a January 2026 interview, Jim Armendariz described his father’s purchase of a ranch outside Cuervo in 2020. In July 2026, Rancho Armendariz of Cuervo became a finalist for the New Mexico Leopold Conservation Award. Ranching belongs to Cuervo’s present as well as its past.[^2]

This book explains how productive country lost much of its town. Cuervo brought together trade, schooling and services for people spread across a wider territory. Its fortunes depended on keeping that work locally useful and supported. Ranching could continue as the reasons to maintain a shop, employ a teacher or finance a newspaper changed.

## Roads and roadside trade

The familiar highway story is simple: Route 66 brought trade, and Interstate 40 took it away. The newer road is much larger than the settlement beside it. Its physical presence makes it easy to imagine one decisive turning point.

Published accounts give that turning point different roles. The National Trust for Historic Preservation identifies interstate construction as the beginning of Cuervo’s decline. Jim Winnerman’s 2022 account in *Wild West* describes earlier railway disappointment. He also says that most residents had left before the interstate arrived. These accounts disagree about whether the new road began the decline or dealt a later blow.[^3]

A road change can hurt businesses in different ways. A filling station may rely on passing drivers. A merchant supplying ranchers may serve customers who deliberately travel there. A hotel needs people with a reason to stay overnight. An interchange keeps a place connected to the road, but it cannot guarantee customers for each business.

At Cuervo, some losses came much earlier. The lumber-stock withdrawal was reported in 1911. In 1922, a newspaper proprietor argued for concentrating publication in Santa Rosa. The population reported for the Cuervo precinct fell sharply during the 1930s. All three events preceded the documented interstate project of the 1960s. The first two even preceded Route 66’s designation in 1926.[^4]

The interstate belongs to a later phase of Cuervo’s decline. It could damage surviving roadside trade, but the history of contraction was already decades old.

## A store with a wide reach

The early merchant offers a clear example. In 1958, Frank H. Grubbs wrote a study of the Bond enterprises. He traced the Cuervo business to a predecessor at Cabra in 1899. The move to Cuervo was complete by the end of 1903. Grubbs connected it with access to the railway.[^5]

The Cuervo firm did several jobs. It sold goods, extended credit and dealt in sheep and wool. In March 1910, the *Clipper* reported fourteen cars of sheep loaded at Cuervo for Kansas. The following week’s issue reported Bond & Wiest unloading separate cars of flour, sugar and grain.[^6] The railway carried agricultural goods out and supplies in.

Much of the work that supported this trade took place outside the village. Ranchers needed access to distant buyers. They also needed goods that arrived from elsewhere. Cuervo brought these transactions together. Its small resident population was only part of the merchant’s market.

Grubbs’s reconstruction of the early accounts shows positive net profits in every year from 1904 through 1915, with recovery after a fall in 1910 and 1911. He found the business still operating in 1958.[^5] The merchant survived changes that ended other local undertakings.

A firm serving a wide territory could prosper with relatively few employees or neighboring businesses. Its success depended on the trade it handled; the town’s growth depended on how much work and spending that trade kept nearby.

## What kept a service open?

The school gave families another reason to come to Cuervo. In September 1910, the *Clipper* reported sixty-seven pupils. It also said that families were moving in from the surrounding country so their children could attend. By April 1912, the paper described a new two-story school, two teachers and further moves for schooling.[^7]

The school helped build its own constituency. Families moved because instruction was available, bringing residents into a place where they would also need housing and supplies.

The newspaper depended partly on a different return. On December 1, 1922, Katharine A. Hicks announced that she was ending her financial connection with the *Clipper*. It had lost money, but she considered its political purpose fulfilled and treated the losses as a campaign contribution. She judged that the county could support one first-class weekly, preferably at Santa Rosa, where her associate Charles Gerhardt planned improvements to the *Public Voice*.[^8] Her announcement dates the withdrawal of her support; the paper’s exact final publication date remains uncertain.

Hicks’s decision joins commercial calculation to political purpose. Potential readers mattered, but so did what the owner wanted her money to achieve. The institutional history of Cuervo turns on such differences.

## Which Cuervo is being counted?

The name Cuervo does not always mean the same area. A photograph may show the village’s buildings. A census table may count residents across a larger district. A merchant’s customers may live farther away still. These differences matter when the sources are compared.

The published population of Cuervo election precinct 6 was 287 in 1930, 188 in 1940 and 210 in 1950. The precinct included land beyond the built settlement. These are therefore counts of the precinct’s residents, including people outside the village. Comparisons with earlier censuses face further problems because reporting areas changed.[^9]

The figures show a large fall followed by a smaller rise. They put population loss before the interstate. They also show that the reported population did not fall in every decade. The increase from 1940 to 1950 was twenty-two people. In a population this small, a few households could noticeably change the total.

The censuses provide observations ten years apart. Arrivals, departures, births and deaths combined between them, while businesses followed their own timetables. A population total records the balance of those movements, not their causes.

Where people live is also different from where they obtain services. A family could remain near Cuervo while traveling elsewhere for school or shopping. The loss of a local service might first change its journeys and spending. A move of residence could come later, or never happen. These are separate changes, even when one influences another.

Railway administration provides another example. Rock Island’s December 1974 station book listed Cuervo without an agent and assigned its billing to Tucumcari. Santa Rosa’s billing was also assigned there.[^10] For this railway function, both towns depended on a center farther east.

The assignment identifies an administrative relationship, not actual freight shipments. It also places a limit on Santa Rosa’s role: for railway billing, the larger neighbor depended on Tucumcari too.

Different services had different centers. Following each connection separately reveals a region organized around overlapping markets and institutions.

This also changes the meaning of local decline. A service can leave a town while remaining available to its residents elsewhere. That can still impose costs: longer journeys, fewer nearby jobs and fewer reasons to visit. Continued access to a service does not mean that its departure left the town unaffected.

## The economics of a small place

A small town survives by assembling enough work and support for its particular activities. That support can come from residents, rural customers, travelers, public funds or an owner pursuing another purpose. Economics helps explain how those sources fit together.

Adam Smith linked specialization to the size of the market. Put simply, a specialist needs enough work to make a living. Where demand is small, one person or firm may perform several tasks. Where demand is larger, separate businesses may be able to support themselves.[^11]

Cuervo gives that idea a concrete setting. Its merchant combined several lines of work and reached customers beyond the village. The relevant market was larger than the local population. This helps explain how a substantial enterprise could operate in a small settlement.

Central-place theory asks how towns supply their surrounding areas with goods and services. Different activities need different numbers of customers and draw them from different distances. A frequently used, inexpensive service may survive on a relatively small market. A costly specialist may need customers from a much wider area.[^11]

Agricultural growth raises a related question: where does the income go? A rancher earning more might buy supplies locally, purchase equipment elsewhere or invest in the ranch. Higher livestock receipts need not produce another shop in Cuervo. Douglass North’s work on agriculture and regional growth explains why exports alone cannot guarantee a more varied local economy. How production is organized and where income is spent also matter.[^11]

Transport can change these choices in several directions. Better connections may help a local merchant reach more customers and obtain cheaper supplies. They may also help those customers reach a larger competing store. Easier, more comfortable journeys can change which trip seems worthwhile. A town can become better connected while losing some of its local trade.

Home air conditioning merits consideration, but it offers little explanation for Cuervo’s loss of services. A window unit could improve comfort in a small house without a central system. Modest savings on cooling elsewhere would offer little reason to bear the expense and disruption of moving. There is no evidence that a cooling advantage drove moves to Santa Rosa. By 1980, about two-thirds of Santa Rosa’s year-round housing reportedly had air conditioning, yet the town’s population was slightly below the 1970 count. Cooling could make residence more comfortable without sustaining the jobs, shops and institutions that made Cuervo a local center.[^12]

Services can also support one another. A school attracts families; those families create demand for housing and trade. Losses can work in the other direction, weakening reasons to maintain other activities. The Cuervo record establishes some of these relationships directly, including moves for schooling. Later feedback remains a possible mechanism rather than a fully traced chain.

The relevant requirements belong to activities. A missing teacher or withdrawn subsidy can matter more immediately than a change in the population total. Replacements, new funding and different ways of operating can restore provision, as Cuervo’s reopenings show.

The chapters that follow explain the formation, maintenance and contraction of that local work. The strongest evidence concerns the arrangements that made Cuervo useful; the later history shows how much of its concentration of services was lost within a continuing regional economy.

## What the buildings can show

The surviving buildings are an important part of the evidence. Photographs show their materials, condition and position in the landscape. They can record damage precisely. They usually cannot date a business’s departure or explain why it closed.

Property records make the same distinction necessary. An assessor card prepared in January 1983 classified a Cuervo property as a gas station, rated its condition poor and used a salvage basis for valuation. A later entry recorded no change in December 2008. The assessment records the property’s condition, leaving its trading history open.[^13]

Other scholars have examined places where visible decay coexists with continued use and attachment. Studies of former mining communities distinguish visitors’ impressions from residents’ experiences. Rural geographers have described several kinds of abandonment and reuse, including declining settlements surrounded by active agriculture.[^14] Cuervo belongs within this wider history.

This book’s contribution is to reconstruct how its particular economy worked. The following chapters examine the town’s formation, trade, services, population and transport. They then bring those findings together to explain its decline. Investment, recovery and survival remain part of that explanation.

Cuervo lost many of the activities that had made it a local center. The surrounding country remained productive and connected to distant markets. Explaining how those histories diverged begins with an earlier question: why did people gather at this particular place?
